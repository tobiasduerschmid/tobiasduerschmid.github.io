# Tutorial exemplars and checked diagnostic examples

Inspected 2 October 2026. Evidence class: **tutorial exemplar**, except the language checks, which are **authoritative semantics**, and the borrowing suggestions, which are **proposed design**. These are bounded inspections of public pages and selected chapters, not complete resource audits. Resource popularity, testimonials, and an author's instructional rationale are not comparative learning-outcome evidence. The eight resources were selected for contrasting audiences, sequences, practice, and tooling; the table is not a ranking.

## Observed resource designs

### Helsinki Haskell MOOC — Joel Kaasinen and John Lång

**Audience:** beginners, with prior programming helpful; exercises require command-line and Git familiarity. **Observed sequence:** expressions/types and recursion; lists/immutability/Maybe/Either; partial application and higher-order functions; folds/classes; algebraic data types; modeling and monoids; a taste of IO. Part 2 adds effects, internals, testing, and applications. **Practice/feedback:** end-of-lecture quizzes, source-file exercises, test output including counterexamples, and instructions to investigate failures in GHCi. Completion thresholds determine grades. **Inspection:** index, Part 1 contents, first quiz/setup/test-feedback sections, selected recursion and partial-application examples. **Risk/inference:** tooling can add incidental load; quiz correctness and exercise completion alone cannot establish independent understanding. [Course index](https://haskell.mooc.fi/), [Part 1, §§1.12–1.14 and 3.2](https://haskell.mooc.fi/part1).

### Haskell Programming from First Principles — Christopher Allen and Julie Moronuki

**Audience:** new programmers and experienced programmers entering Haskell. **Observed sequence:** advertised foundations begin with lambda calculus, expressions/strings/basic data, then types/classes, recursion/lists/folds, algebraic data, projects/testing, and abstractions; operational non-strictness and IO receive later chapters. **Practice/feedback:** the public sample explicitly asks readers to predict in their heads before checking in GHCi, parenthesize expressions, rewrite bindings, and modify small functions. REPL comparison supplies immediate behavioral feedback. **Inspection:** official contents/rationale and selected chapter 2 exercises in the public sample; later chapters were not inspected. **Risk/inference:** the opening calculus chapter was unavailable in the sample, so its learning burden cannot be judged here; author claims about spaced repetition are intentions, not measured outcomes. [Official site](https://haskellbook.com/), [sample, chapter 2, §§2.10–2.11](https://haskellbook.com/img/sample.pdf).

### Programming in Haskell, second edition — Graham Hutton

**Audience:** explicitly requires no programming experience. **Observed sequence:** types/classes, defining functions, comprehensions, recursion, higher-order functions, declared types, and the Countdown case study precede interactive programming; monads/parsing/foldables precede the dedicated lazy-evaluation and reasoning chapters. **Practice/feedback:** the author describes chapter exercises, extended examples, selected solutions, lecture videos/slides/code, and instructor exam materials. **Inspection:** author's edition page and table of contents only; exercise texts, solutions, lectures, and learner outcomes were not inspected. **Risk/inference:** a concise book may require instructor mediation for learners lacking prerequisites; the late dedicated laziness chapter does not imply laziness is entirely absent earlier. This resource is retained as a contrasting curriculum map, with weaker inspection depth. [Author's edition page and contents](https://people.cs.nott.ac.uk/pszgmh/pih.html).

### University of Pennsylvania CIS 194, Spring 2013 — Brent Yorgey

**Audience:** university introductory Haskell course; prior-experience prerequisites were not verified on the inspected pages. **Observed sequence:** introduction, algebraic data types, recursion/polymorphism, higher-order functions/type inference, classes, laziness, folds/monoids, IO, then functors/applicatives/monads. **Practice/feedback:** weekly homework and an open-ended final project; published policy separates correctness/style credit and allows revisions. Homework 4 asks students to replace explicit recursion with pipelines and implement folds on lists/trees. The laziness lecture contrasts demand on an outer constructor with demand on its fields. **Inspection:** course description, schedule, policies, homework 4, and selected laziness/IO lecture sections. **Risk/inference:** archived material needs compatibility checks for current GHC; instructor feedback and the actual difficulty experienced by learners are unobserved. [Schedule](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~cis1940/spring13/lectures.html), [homework 4](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~cis1940/spring13/hw/04-higher-order.pdf), [revision policy](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~cis1940/spring13/policies.html), [laziness](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~cis1940/spring13/lectures/06-laziness.html).

### Learn You a Haskell for Great Good! — Miran Lipovača; maintained community fork

**Audience:** explicitly targets imperative-language programmers new to functional programming. **Observed sequence:** functions/lists, types/classes, function syntax, recursion, higher-order functions, modules, declared types, and later IO/abstractions. **Practice/feedback:** inspected higher-order chapter uses GHCi transcripts, partial-application transformations, function-type explanations, and a diagnostic explanation of trying to print a function. **Inspection:** author-authorized community fork's introduction, contents, and selected higher-order sections; original domain failed to fetch. The fork documents its provenance. **Risk/inference:** exposition and successful transcript reproduction can permit passive reading; independent graded practice was not visible in inspected sections. Informal metaphors and claims about evaluation efficiency need qualification when reused. [Fork provenance](https://learnyouahaskell.github.io/), [introduction](https://learnyouahaskell.github.io/introduction.html), [higher-order chapter](https://learnyouahaskell.github.io/higher-order-functions.html).

### Haskell Wikibook — Wikibooks contributors

**Audience:** programming beginners and experienced programmers, with a warning that existing habits may interfere. **Observed sequence:** beginner track includes bindings/types/lists and simple IO before recursion/map/folds; later sections address declared types, pattern matching, advanced abstractions, and practical reference topics. **Practice/feedback:** Lists III asks readers to implement recursion then convert it to folds, choose a fold for reverse, and reason about noncommutative subtraction; linked solutions enable comparison. Simple IO contrasts actions, resulting values, and let bindings. **Inspection:** contents and selected Lists III/Simple input and output sections. **Risk/inference:** multiple routes mix learning-path and reference roles; readers need guidance to avoid skipping prerequisites. Explanations of fold direction should be paired with explicit association and demand, which Lists III itself discusses. [Contents](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Haskell), [Lists III](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Haskell/Lists_III), [simple IO](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Haskell/Simple_input_and_output).

### CodeWorld — project contributors

**Audience:** mathematics instruction and school use, with a distinct standard-Haskell mode. **Observed progression:** API entry points support drawing, time-dependent animation, and event-driven activities; this is an affordance progression, not a verified course sequence. **Practice/feedback:** browser editor/compiler, visual execution, sharing, and a student help forum are described by the project; API examples show a complete small drawing. **Inspection:** project README, live application's exposed controls, and standard-Haskell API entry points; guide pages failed to fetch, so exercises and assessment were not inspected. **Risk/inference:** CodeWorld's educational language/library variant must not be equated with standard Haskell. Visual correctness alone can hide misunderstanding and needs accessible alternative output. [Project variants](https://github.com/google/codeworld), [standard-Haskell API examples](https://code.world/doc-haskell/CodeWorld.html), [educational core types](https://code.world/doc/Core.html).

### Exercism Haskell — Exercism contributors

**Audience:** people practicing Haskell; inspected landing page did not establish programming prerequisites. **Observed sequence:** a practice track with exercises unlocked through progression; no fixed introductory concept curriculum was established. **Practice/feedback:** platform advertises automatic analysis and volunteer mentoring toward idiomatic Haskell. The public Leap exercise states calendar rules, contrasting cases, and a named function to implement. **Inspection:** track landing page, exercise listing, and public Leap instructions; no logged-in solution/mentoring session, test runner, or analysis result was observed. **Risk/inference:** automated correctness, idiomatic style, and conceptual explanation are different outcomes; mentor availability/quality and delayed learning are unverified. [Track](https://exercism.org/tracks/haskell), [practice listing](https://exercism.org/tracks/haskell/exercises), [Leap exercise](https://exercism.org/tracks/haskell/exercises/leap).

## Original borrowing proposals, to evaluate with learners

These proposals are reviewer synthesis, not claims that the resources caused learning gains.

| Resource | Proposed borrowing | Learning check beyond reproduction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Helsinki | Put a conceptual checkpoint beside a small executable exercise and a useful counterexample. | Explain why the counterexample fails; repair a related unseen function. |
| Haskellbook | Ask for predictions before REPL execution; progressively modify and then independently construct functions. | Explain a prediction error and solve a different instance without the transcript. |
| Hutton | Use a pure-language foundation followed by a substantial case study that integrates earlier skills. | Assess which representation/recursion scheme fits a new specification. |
| CIS 194 | Combine recursion-to-pipeline transformations, constructor-demand traces, and revisable submissions. | Grade explanations separately from code correctness; revisit after a delay. |
| Learn You a Haskell | Use concrete partial-application/type transformations and explain why a printing error is not a failed function definition. | Infer the remaining function type and apply it to an unseen input. |
| Wikibook | Require both recursive and fold formulations, then discriminate among alternative folds using subtraction. | Predict grouping and demanded subexpressions for a new combining function. |
| CodeWorld | Offer a small zero-install pure-function project with visible output, using standard Haskell when that is the course target. | Explain the function model and provide equivalent textual/keyboard-accessible checks. |
| Exercism | Pair specification-based independent problems with review of idiomatic alternatives. | Ask for justification of edge cases and transfer to a changed specification. |

## Twelve diagnostic examples checked against authoritative semantics

These are **proposed diagnostic items**, not empirically established prevalent misconceptions. Correct answers were derived from inspected specification/library definitions; no compiler run was performed. Numeric examples can be specialized to `Integer` to avoid unrelated defaulting questions. Distinguish producing a value, demanding it, and displaying it. Do not implement unbounded divergent runs in a learner's browser.

| # | Diagnostic expression or contrast | Checked answer and target distinction | Authority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `f x y` versus `f (x y)` | Application groups as `(f x) y`; the second expression uses `x y` as the argument to `f`. | [Report §3.3](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch3.html) |
| 2 | `sub :: Int -> Int -> Int`; `sub x y = x-y`; type of `sub 10` | Remaining type is `Int -> Int`; arrows describe a function returning another function. | [Report §4.1.2](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch4.html) and [§3.3](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch3.html) |
| 3 | `pairSub (x,y) = x-y` versus `sub x y = x-y` | A pair argument and two curried applications have different types; `sub (10,3)` is ill-typed under item 2's signature. | [Report §6.1.4](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch6.html) |
| 4 | `foldl (-) 0 [1,2,3]`; `foldr (-) 0 [1,2,3]` | `-6` versus `2`: `((0-1)-2)-3` versus `1-(2-(3-0))`. Association differs; this alone does not specify runtime evaluation order. | [Report Prelude fold definitions](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch9.html) |
| 5 | `foldr const 0 [1..] :: Integer`; `foldl const 0 [1..] :: Integer` | First yields `1`; demanding the second diverges because `foldl` needs the list's end. `foldr` does not necessarily begin evaluation at the last element. | [Report Prelude `foldr`, `foldl`, `const`](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch9.html) |
| 6 | `length [undefined :: Int]` versus `head [undefined :: Int]` | Length yields `1`; demanding the head raises the undefined error. Traversing the list spine need not evaluate its elements. | [Report Prelude `length` and `head`](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch9.html), [non-strict application](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch6.html) |
| 7 | `const 42 (error "boom") :: Integer` | Yields `42`; the discarded argument is not needed. Lazy demand differs from evaluating every argument before a call. | [Report `const`](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch9.html), [§6.2](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch6.html) |
| 8 | ``Just (undefined :: Int) `seq` 1`` versus ``(undefined :: Maybe Int) `seq` 1`` | First yields `1`; demanding the second raises an error. Constructor availability differs from availability of its field. | [Report §6.2](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch6.html), [GHC-bundled deepseq documentation](https://downloads.haskell.org/~ghc/7.10.3/docs/html/libraries/deepseq-1.4.1.1/src/Control-DeepSeq.html) |
| 9 | ``[1,undefined :: Int] `seq` 3`` versus ``[1,undefined :: Int] `deepseq` 3`` | Shallow forcing yields `3`; deep forcing raises an error with the standard list/Int `NFData` instances. Import `Control.DeepSeq`; do not generalize to arbitrarily defined instances. | [GHC-bundled deepseq documentation and instances](https://downloads.haskell.org/~ghc/7.10.3/docs/html/libraries/deepseq-1.4.1.1/src/Control-DeepSeq.html) |
| 10 | In `do`: `let action = getLine` versus `name <- getLine` | First binds an `IO String` action; second sequences it and binds its resulting `String` for later statements. | [Report §7.1](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch7.html), [§3.14 translation](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch3.html) |
| 11 | `main = do { return (); putStrLn "after" }` | Prints `after`. `return` supplies an action result; it does not exit the enclosing `do` block. | [Report §6.3.6 monad laws](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch6.html), [§3.14](https://www.haskell.org/onlinereport/haskell2010/haskellch3.html) |
| 12 | Enter `(return 3 :: IO Int)` at GHCi | GHCi executes the action and displays `3`; this interface behavior does not make `IO Int` identical to `Int`. | [GHCi §3.4.1](https://downloads.haskell.org/ghc/latest/docs/users_guide/ghci.html#i-o-actions-at-the-prompt) |

**Verification boundaries:** Haskell 2010 specifies these core semantics; its class hierarchy and pattern-failure translation predate modern GHC's Applicative/Monad and MonadFail changes. The examples above do not rely on the old hierarchy or failing `do` patterns. The deepseq page is an explicitly versioned historical GHC-bundled library source (1.4.1.1), not a claim about the latest package release. It includes the required standard list/Int instances. Current GHCi documentation was inspected at the moving `latest` URL and identified itself as GHC 9.14.1; use a version-pinned link in any published teaching material. A reduction illustration is a semantic explanation, not a promise of GHC's exact optimization, allocation, or execution trace.

**Terminology caution:** actions themselves are Haskell values. The teaching distinction is between an action of type `IO a` and its resulting value of type `a`; saying “IO is not a value” would introduce an error. Likewise, no numerical claim about how many students confuse these examples is supported by tutorial inspection.
