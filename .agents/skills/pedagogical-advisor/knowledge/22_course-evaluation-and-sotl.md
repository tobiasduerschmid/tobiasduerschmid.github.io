# Course Evaluation and Scholarship of Teaching and Learning

## Improvement stance

Treat a course as a design hypothesis:
- intended mechanism;
- implementation;
- evidence;
- interpretation;
- revision.

Evaluation is not limited to end-of-course ratings.

## Questions

Examples:
- Did cumulative retrieval improve retention?
- Which misconception persisted?
- Did a new rubric improve evaluative judgment?
- Did group roles distribute participation?
- Did GenAI guardrails preserve independent performance?
- Which students benefited or were burdened?
- Did workload match estimates?

## Evidence sources

- diagnostic and summative performance;
- item-level patterns;
- transfer tasks;
- drafts and revisions;
- concept maps;
- observations;
- student explanations;
- participation data;
- time-on-task;
- office-hour/help requests;
- student surveys/interviews;
- peer review of materials;
- instructor reflection;
- retention/persistence.

Triangulate.

## Rapid cycle

1. identify a priority problem;
2. define a measurable design change;
3. predict mechanism;
4. collect baseline;
5. implement;
6. check fidelity;
7. analyze learning and burden;
8. disaggregate;
9. revise;
10. document.

## Student feedback

Use midcourse feedback for:
- clarity;
- pacing;
- workload;
- access;
- climate;
- usability;
- perceived bottlenecks.

Close the loop by telling students what will change, what will not, and why.

Ratings of enjoyment or teaching quality should not be treated as direct learning measures and may contain bias.

## Peer review

Ask a colleague to examine:
- alignment;
- cognitive demand;
- assessment sampling;
- workload;
- accessibility;
- instructions;
- rubrics;
- lecture evidence.

Observation is more useful when focused on a specific question and followed promptly by discussion.

## Ethical SoTL

Consider:
- consent and institutional review;
- privacy;
- power relationships;
- data security;
- equity;
- conflict of interest;
- reporting of negative results;
- intervention risk.

Course improvement data are not automatically research data.

## Causal caution

A pre/post gain can reflect maturation, testing, selection, or scoring. A comparison across cohorts can reflect many differences. Use cautious language and, where feasible, stronger designs.

## Documentation

Maintain a design decision log:
- problem;
- evidence;
- change;
- rationale;
- expected effect;
- data;
- result;
- next decision.

Use `templates/design-decision-log.md` and `templates/course-evaluation-plan.md`.
