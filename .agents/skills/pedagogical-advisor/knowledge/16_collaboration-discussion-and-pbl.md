# Collaboration, Discussion, Problem-Based and Project-Based Learning

## Collaborative learning

Small-group learning can improve achievement, attitudes, and persistence, especially when interaction is structured. The mechanism is not proximity; it is explanation, comparison, coordination, and shared regulation.

Design ingredients:
- positive interdependence;
- individual accountability;
- promotive interaction;
- explicit collaboration skills;
- group processing;
- meaningful shared task;
- instructor monitoring.

## Discussion

A productive discussion has:
- a question worth resolving;
- preparation and evidence;
- participation structure;
- norms;
- moves for clarification, challenge, and uptake;
- an intellectual product;
- synthesis.

Useful moves:
- “What evidence supports that?”
- “Who can restate the distinction?”
- “What assumption differs?”
- “What would change your view?”
- “How does this connect to the text/data/model?”
- “Which claim can the group endorse, qualify, or reject?”

## Group formation

Choose deliberately:
- random for low-stakes mixing;
- heterogeneous for complementary resources;
- homogeneous for targeted support;
- self-selected when trust or project ownership matters.

Rotate groups only when benefits exceed coordination costs. Stable groups can develop norms; rotating groups broaden networks.

## Roles

Roles help when they represent intellectual functions:
- facilitator;
- evidence checker;
- skeptic;
- connector;
- recorder/synthesizer;
- process monitor.

Avoid roles that permanently assign low-status administrative work.

## Individual assurance

Use:
- individual prework;
- random spokesperson;
- individual reflection;
- oral check;
- personal section;
- individual quiz;
- peer contribution evidence;
- rotating roles.

A group grade alone cannot support an individual competence claim.

## Problem-based learning

A PBL cycle:
1. encounter problem;
2. identify facts;
3. generate hypotheses;
4. identify knowledge gaps;
5. conduct self-directed learning;
6. apply new knowledge;
7. evaluate and reflect.

PBL works best with:
- carefully designed problems;
- facilitator scaffolding;
- access to resources;
- clear outcomes;
- integration across disciplines;
- assessment aligned with reasoning and learning process.

Novices need more guidance than experts.

## Project-based learning

Projects organize learning around an extended product or investigation. Strong projects:
- address a driving question;
- require important disciplinary knowledge;
- include milestones;
- use critique and revision;
- involve authentic audiences or constraints;
- retain individual learning evidence;
- include reflection on decisions.

A project can become production work dominated by students who already possess the skills. Teach and assess the learning within the project.

## Collaboration online

Provide:
- small groups;
- clear channels;
- response expectations;
- asynchronous preparation;
- shared artifacts;
- facilitation prompts;
- role and deadline visibility;
- contingency for nonparticipation;
- privacy and accessibility.

Discussion boards fail when prompts invite isolated mini-essays with no need to engage peers.

## Conflict and safety

Establish:
- critique ideas, not persons;
- use evidence;
- ask before assuming;
- acknowledge uncertainty;
- repair harm;
- instructor intervention thresholds;
- routes for private reporting.

Psychological safety does not mean avoidance of intellectual challenge.

## Evidence stance

- Structured small-group learning: **A/B**.
- Peer explanation and argumentation: **B**.
- PBL/PjBL: **B**, dependent on guidance, task quality, and assessment.
- Unstructured group work: **D**.
- Minimally guided problem solving for novices: **X** as a general default.
