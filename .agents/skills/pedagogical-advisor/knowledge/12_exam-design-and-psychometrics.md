# Exam Design and Classroom Psychometrics

## Build a blueprint before questions

Define:
- purpose and stakes;
- outcomes;
- content domains;
- cognitive processes;
- weighting;
- item formats;
- difficulty distribution;
- time;
- allowed resources;
- individual versus collaborative evidence;
- accessibility;
- security and GenAI conditions;
- scoring and review.

A blueprint protects against overtesting easy-to-write content.

## Cognitive demand

Assess:
- retrieval of essential knowledge;
- explanation;
- application;
- discrimination among methods;
- analysis of evidence;
- evaluation;
- synthesis;
- transfer.

Do not infer higher-order thinking from an essay format alone. A well-designed multiple-choice item can assess application; a poorly designed essay can assess recall.

## Selected-response items

Useful for breadth and efficient scoring. Good multiple-choice items:
- target an important outcome;
- present a clear stem;
- avoid irrelevant complexity;
- have one defensible best answer;
- use plausible distractors based on reasoning errors;
- avoid clues, grammatical mismatch, and trick wording;
- avoid “all of the above” when it weakens inference;
- keep options parallel;
- require computation or reasoning when intended.

Ask students to justify selected responses in practice or sampled items when reasoning matters.

## Constructed response

Use when evidence requires:
- organization;
- explanation;
- derivation;
- argument;
- design;
- interpretation;
- communication.

Specify criteria and expected scope. Use structured prompts to improve sampling and scoring reliability.

## Oral exams

Oral assessment allows probing and adaptive follow-up. Risks include anxiety, inconsistency, and bias. Use:
- standardized core prompts;
- follow-up rules;
- scoring criteria;
- examiner calibration;
- recording or second marking for high stakes;
- preparation information;
- accommodations.

## Open-book exams

Open-book does not automatically create higher-order assessment. Students need:
- tasks requiring selection and use;
- familiarity with resources;
- time appropriate to search demands;
- explicit allowed materials;
- practice in the format.

Core knowledge may still need retrieval because searching consumes time and attention.

## Take-home exams and GenAI

Assume access to tools and other people unless controlled. Decide whether the claim is:
- tool-mediated performance;
- independent performance;
- verification and critique;
- process capability.

Use:
- personalized or contextualized data;
- process logs;
- oral defense;
- staged submissions;
- source verification;
- reflection on decisions;
- controlled complementary evidence.

Do not interpret a take-home product as independent capability without supporting evidence.

## Time and speed

Time pressure should reflect the construct. Speed can introduce construct-irrelevant variance. Estimate time by having knowledgeable but nonauthor solvers complete the exam and multiply appropriately. Provide accessible timing and breaks where needed.

## Scoring

Develop:
- answer key;
- point rationale;
- partial-credit rules;
- rubric;
- exemplars;
- moderation process;
- handling of alternative valid answers.

Score blind where feasible. Separate substantive performance from handwriting, accent, or formatting unless relevant.

## Item review

Before use:
- map to blueprint;
- verify accuracy;
- check ambiguity;
- inspect bias and language;
- solve independently;
- estimate difficulty;
- confirm time;
- inspect overlap and cueing;
- check accessibility;
- verify media and equations;
- review AI vulnerability relative to policy.

## Post-exam analysis

For objective items:
- difficulty;
- discrimination;
- distractor functioning;
- omissions;
- subgroup patterns;
- timing;
- reliability estimates when appropriate.

For constructed responses:
- score distributions;
- inter-rater agreement;
- common reasoning patterns;
- rubric functioning;
- moderation notes.

Do not delete an item solely because high performers missed it; investigate content, teaching, ambiguity, and scoring.

## Security and integrity

Use proportionate controls:
- equivalent forms;
- item-bank rotation;
- randomized values;
- secure administration;
- clear rules;
- identity assurance;
- supervised components;
- process evidence.

Security cannot repair invalid tasks.

## Cumulative exams

Cumulative assessment supports spaced retrieval and course integration. Balance:
- core recurring knowledge;
- recent learning;
- integrated questions;
- transparent scope;
- practice opportunities.

## Fairness

Check:
- language complexity;
- cultural assumptions;
- disability access;
- technology;
- time;
- content opportunity to learn;
- stereotype cues;
- scoring consistency;
- appeals and corrections.

## Exam quality gate

- Does the blueprint represent the course?
- Did students practice the assessed thinking?
- Are stakes proportional to evidence quality?
- Is there enough sampling?
- Is time reasonable?
- Are scoring rules dependable?
- Are access and fairness addressed?
- Does GenAI access alter the inference?
- Will results inform future learning?
