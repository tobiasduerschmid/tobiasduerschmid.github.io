# Evidence Reasoning for Pedagogical Decisions

Evidence-based teaching is not the mechanical application of a ranked list. It is disciplined reasoning from research, theory, context, constraints, and local evidence.

## Evidence question first

Define the claim before searching:
- Which learners?
- Which discipline and knowledge type?
- Which intervention, compared with what?
- At what dose and fidelity?
- Which outcome: immediate performance, retention, transfer, grades, failure, persistence, motivation, equity, or perception?
- Over what time?
- Under what conditions?

A study showing higher in-class accuracy does not necessarily show unaided learning. A study showing student preference does not establish achievement. A technique that improves recall may or may not improve transfer.

## Evidence hierarchy is not enough

Randomized trials and meta-analyses are valuable, but quality depends on:
- construct validity;
- comparison condition;
- attrition and missing data;
- measurement quality;
- intervention fidelity;
- publication bias;
- sample and setting;
- analytic choices;
- outcome timing;
- researcher independence;
- heterogeneity and moderators.

A systematic review of weak studies can still yield a weak conclusion. A strong field experiment may be more useful than a broad but indirect synthesis.

## Evidence categories used by this skill

### A — Strong and replicated
Use when convergent evidence supports a practice across credible settings and the mechanism matches the task. Examples: retrieval practice for retention, distributed practice, worked examples for novice acquisition in well-structured domains, guided active learning in undergraduate STEM.

### B — Supported but conditional
Use when effects depend strongly on design and context. Examples: feedback, interleaving, collaborative learning, inquiry, problem-based learning, rubrics, belonging interventions, utility-value interventions.

### C — Promising or emerging
Use for new technology or practices with limited field evidence. Examples: many GenAI tutoring and assessment designs.

### D — Contested or insufficient
Use when high-quality reviews disagree or effects are small and unstable. Examples: broad claims that growth-mindset interventions reliably raise achievement for all students.

### X — Disconfirmed or misapplied
Examples: matching teaching to learning-style preferences; treating unguided discovery as a general default for novices; assuming a universal 10-minute attention span; relying on AI detectors as definitive evidence.

## Effect sizes: interpret, do not worship

Effect sizes depend on:
- outcome scale and reliability;
- control-group practice;
- study design;
- unit of analysis;
- duration;
- baseline risk;
- implementation quality;
- subject and age.

An average standardized effect does not tell you:
- whether the intervention is feasible;
- whether it benefits all groups;
- whether it improves transfer;
- how much implementation is required;
- what it should replace;
- whether the comparison was poor practice.

Use absolute outcomes where possible, such as failure rates, percentage-point gains, time saved, or changes in distributions.

## Heterogeneity is substantive information

When effects vary, ask why:
- learner prior knowledge;
- task complexity;
- class size;
- instructor training;
- intensity;
- accountability;
- feedback quality;
- duration;
- measurement type;
- disciplinary norms;
- implementation fidelity.

For example, active learning is not one treatment. Its effects depend on the quality of questions, student accountability, peer interaction, instructor response, and alignment with assessment.

## Mechanism and boundary conditions

A recommendation is stronger when:
- the proposed mechanism is plausible;
- the design actually activates that mechanism;
- evidence shows the intended outcome;
- moderators are understood.

Example: “Use group work” is underspecified. A stronger claim is: structured peer explanation with individual commitment, comparison of reasoning, instructor diagnosis, and whole-class synthesis can produce constructive and interactive processing. Unstructured division of labor may not.

## Triangulation

Prefer convergence among:
- controlled research;
- field studies;
- qualitative process evidence;
- learning-science theory;
- student work;
- classroom observation;
- assessment data;
- learner feedback;
- implementation data.

Student perceptions are valuable for workload, climate, clarity, and usability, but should be triangulated with learning evidence.

## Local evaluation

Even an A-level practice can fail locally. Include:
- baseline or preassessment;
- implementation checks;
- embedded formative evidence;
- comparison across iterations;
- disaggregated outcomes;
- student work samples;
- workload data;
- unintended consequences.

Avoid claiming causality from a single course iteration. Use local evidence for improvement, not exaggerated proof.

## Conflicting reviews

When credible sources disagree:
1. state the disagreement;
2. compare inclusion criteria and study quality;
3. distinguish overall average from subgroup hypotheses;
4. avoid cherry-picking;
5. recommend the lower-risk use;
6. propose local evaluation.

Growth-mindset reviews illustrate this: some identify small, targeted benefits; others argue apparent achievement effects are explained by bias and weak design. The prudent educational use is to create a learning-oriented climate and teach effective strategies, not to expect a short mindset message to repair structural or instructional problems.

## Meta-meta-analysis cautions

Syntheses such as *Visible Learning* are useful for asking which broad influences deserve attention, but effect-size rankings should not be treated as a menu:
- meta-analyses differ in quality and age;
- constructs overlap;
- outcomes and comparison groups differ;
- average effects obscure moderators;
- “above the hinge point” is not a causal threshold;
- feasible implementation is not specified.

Use such syntheses to generate hypotheses, then consult direct evidence.

## Language calibration

Use:
- “Strong evidence supports…”
- “Evidence generally supports…, especially when…”
- “This is promising but the evidence is still emerging…”
- “Findings are mixed…”
- “This common claim is not supported…”

Avoid:
- “proven to work for everyone”;
- “the best method” without a defined outcome and comparison;
- “brain-based” as a quality label;
- causal wording for correlational data;
- precise effect-size forecasts for a new course.

## Updating evidence

For fields that change rapidly:
- search primary and authoritative sources;
- check publication and update dates;
- prefer official standards and reports for current policy;
- verify whether later replication or critique changed interpretation;
- add the claim to the evidence matrix with an access date.

## Evidence provenance and AI outcome distinctions

A–X are editorial confidence labels for specific claims, not a formal evidence-grading instrument or universal ranking. Record intervention, population, comparator, outcome, version/date, and access scope. Distinguish evidence type from confidence: a normative framework has no causal treatment effect to grade.

For AI, keep assisted performance, independent immediate learning, delayed retention, transfer, self-report, workload, and generated-material quality separate. Check whether the observation is a student, session, tutor, or model output before reporting sample size. A significant effect in one group and not another does not prove between-group difference; nonsignificance does not establish equivalence. Do not double-count a preprint and its publication or several books citing one trial.
