# Cognitive Load, Worked Examples, and Multimedia

## Cognitive architecture

Working memory is sharply limited when processing novel, interacting information. Long-term memory contains organized knowledge that expands functional capacity. Instruction should help learners construct and automate useful schemas without wasting attention.

## Sources of demand

Use contemporary terminology carefully:
- **Intrinsic complexity:** interaction among elements inherent to the target, relative to prior knowledge.
- **Extraneous demand:** processing caused by poor design that does not serve learning.
- **Germane/generative processing:** learner effort directed toward sense-making and schema construction; modern accounts debate whether this is a separate load.

The practical aim is to reduce unnecessary demand and support productive processing.

## Worked-example effect

For novices learning well-structured procedures, studying worked solutions is often more efficient than solving equivalent problems unaided.

Effective examples:
- include the problem, steps, decisions, and result;
- integrate text with the relevant diagram or notation;
- signal goals and subgoals;
- explain why, not only what;
- vary examples to reveal structure;
- prompt self-explanation;
- compare correct and flawed examples.

Transition through:
1. full examples;
2. completion problems;
3. faded steps;
4. independent problems;
5. mixed and transfer problems.

## Expertise reversal

Support that helps novices can hinder more knowledgeable learners by becoming redundant. Adjust based on demonstrated expertise:
- remove obvious explanations;
- permit faster paths;
- increase problem solving;
- use error diagnosis and open tasks;
- allow choice of support;
- fade prompts.

Do not use one fixed level of scaffolding for all students throughout the course.

## Split attention and integration

When learners must mentally integrate separate sources that are mutually dependent, learning suffers. Place labels, explanations, and visuals close to the relevant elements. Avoid forcing learners to switch repeatedly between:
- diagram and distant legend;
- problem and separate formula sheet;
- code and detached commentary;
- image and unrelated paragraph.

## Redundancy

More information is not always better. Redundant on-screen text that duplicates narration can overload visual processing, especially with complex graphics. Use:
- concise headings or key terms;
- narration with meaningful visuals;
- full text when reading itself is required, accessibility demands it, or learners need self-paced review;
- transcripts as optional resources rather than simultaneous duplication.

Context matters; do not apply multimedia principles mechanically.

## Modality

Distributing mutually dependent information across auditory and visual channels can help under some conditions. It can also create transience or accessibility problems. Prefer:
- learner control;
- captions/transcripts;
- segmentation;
- stable reference material;
- alternatives for hearing, language, and processing needs.

## Signaling and coherence

Signal:
- hierarchy;
- causal links;
- steps;
- current position;
- differences;
- takeaways.

Remove:
- decorative stories or images unrelated to the goal;
- animations without explanatory function;
- visual clutter;
- competing highlights;
- excessive slide templates and logos.

“Interesting” additions may distract from the intended model.

## Segmenting and pretraining

Segment complex explanations into learner-manageable units and teach essential components or vocabulary before the full system. Preserve a map of the whole so decomposition does not create disconnected fragments.

## Transient information

Speech and animation disappear. For complex material:
- allow pause/replay;
- provide a stable diagram or summary;
- slow critical steps;
- break animations;
- ask learners to reconstruct;
- avoid narrating long lists that must be compared.

## Example–problem pairs

A robust sequence:
- study example;
- self-explain;
- solve a similar problem;
- compare solution;
- correct;
- then vary surface features or principle.

## Productive load versus simplicity

Do not minimize all effort. Learning requires retrieval, explanation, comparison, and integration. The goal is not effortless instruction; it is to remove effort that does not contribute to the target.

## Slide audit

For each slide:
- What is the learning function?
- What should students attend to?
- Does every element serve that function?
- Are dependent elements integrated?
- Is text readable and concise?
- Is narration duplicative?
- Does the slide remain long enough?
- Is there an accessible alternative?
- What student action follows?

## Evidence stance

- Worked examples for novices in well-structured domains: **A**.
- Fading/completion problems: **A/B**.
- Split-attention, signaling, and redundancy principles: **A/B**, moderated by task and expertise.
- “More media is more engaging and therefore more effective”: **X**.
- Universal application of a single modality rule: **D/X**.
