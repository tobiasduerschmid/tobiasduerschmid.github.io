# Generative AI in Teaching and Assessment

Evidence and capability change rapidly. This is the conceptual overview. For the focused 2026-09-05 evidence update and its limits use `references/ai-learning-evidence-2026.md`, `references/cs-ai-evidence-2026.md`, and `knowledge/25_ai-literacy-and-learning-safeguards.md`. Verify current policy and research when consequential to the request.

## Design stance

GenAI can:
- generate explanations, examples, questions, code, and feedback;
- customize contexts;
- support brainstorming and revision;
- reduce low-level production costs;
- simulate audiences or cases.

It can also:
- provide incorrect or fabricated content;
- allow bypass of productive cognitive work;
- create an illusion of competence;
- obscure authorship and process;
- amplify bias;
- expose private data;
- homogenize output;
- undermine assessment claims.

The relevant question is not “allow or ban AI?” but “What human capability should develop, what role may AI play, and what evidence will show that capability?”

## Capability map

Classify outcomes:

1. **Unaided foundational capability**
   - knowledge and skills needed when AI is absent, wrong, slow, or inappropriate.
2. **AI-augmented capability**
   - using AI while verifying, adapting, and taking responsibility.
3. **AI-evaluative capability**
   - detecting errors, bias, limitations, and evidential weakness.
4. **AI-design capability**
   - selecting workflows, prompts, tools, and guardrails.
5. **Human responsibility in relational/ethical practice**
   - judgment, accountability, care, consent, and context-sensitive decisions.

Assess each accordingly.

## Assignment-specific policy matrix

For each task state:
- prohibited uses;
- permitted uses;
- required uses;
- disclosure format;
- verification obligations;
- data/privacy rules;
- citation/attribution;
- retained process evidence;
- independent components;
- consequences of misuse.

Course-wide slogans are insufficient.

## Preserve productive cognitive effort

Guardrails can require AI to:
- ask questions before answering;
- provide hints;
- reveal one step at a time;
- request the learner’s attempt;
- critique reasoning;
- generate analogous practice;
- withhold final answers;
- ask for explanation and verification.

A large field experiment in high-school mathematics found that unrestricted GPT access improved supported practice performance but harmed later unaided performance, whereas a safeguarded tutor mitigated the harm. Treat tool design as instruction design. In that study, the guarded tutor did not demonstrate a positive independent exam effect; the observed harm was mitigated. Other settings have positive and null results. See AI26-BAS and contrasting studies in `references/ai-learning-evidence-2026.md`.

## Process evidence

Use:
- proposals;
- outlines;
- drafts;
- version histories;
- prompt/output logs;
- source audits;
- decision memos;
- error corrections;
- oral defenses;
- live demonstrations;
- reflection linked to artifacts;
- comparison of AI and human approaches.

Process evidence should support learning and assurance, not become surveillance theater.

## Assessment reform

Current tertiary guidance emphasizes:
- program-level as well as unit-level assurance;
- authentic demonstrations of capability;
- evaluative judgment, critical thinking, and ethical reasoning;
- responsible AI use;
- multiple complementary evidence points;
- reduced reliance on detection.

Use a balanced system:
- controlled/observable evidence;
- authentic AI-integrated performance;
- process evidence;
- oral or interactive follow-up;
- cumulative evidence across a program.

## Avoid “AI-proofing”

Model capabilities improve. Designing tasks around temporary model weaknesses is not sustainable. Instead:
- assess decisions and justification;
- require local/contextual evidence;
- include verification;
- use incomplete or evolving problems;
- incorporate stakeholder interaction;
- require adaptation after feedback;
- assess process and oral reasoning;
- retain independent demonstrations.

## AI for educator work

Use AI to:
- brainstorm variants;
- draft examples;
- generate distractors for review;
- create formative questions;
- adapt tone or format;
- produce starting rubrics;
- summarize anonymous patterns;
- plan scenarios.

The educator must:
- verify content;
- check bias and accessibility;
- protect student data;
- align with outcomes;
- review difficulty;
- document use where required;
- retain professional responsibility.

## AI feedback

AI feedback may increase availability but can be generic, inaccurate, or overconfident. Use:
- constrained criteria;
- retrieval of course resources;
- human review;
- student verification;
- prompts that ask questions;
- comparison with exemplars;
- escalation for uncertainty.

Teach students to treat feedback as a claim requiring evaluation.

## Academic integrity

Do not rely on AI detectors as definitive proof. Use:
- clear rules;
- educational conversations;
- proportionate investigation;
- multiple evidence sources;
- procedural fairness;
- assessment redesign.

## Equity

Consider:
- unequal access to paid models;
- language benefits and harms;
- disability access;
- bias;
- data extraction;
- environmental and institutional costs;
- differential AI literacy.

Provide institutionally supported alternatives and do not make premium access an unstated prerequisite.

## Student AI literacy

Teach:
- how models generate outputs;
- hallucination;
- source verification;
- prompt and context effects;
- bias;
- privacy;
- copyright and attribution;
- uncertainty;
- appropriate reliance;
- comparison of automation and augmentation;
- accountability.
