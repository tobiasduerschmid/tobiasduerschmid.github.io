# Assessment Foundations

Assessment is a process of eliciting, interpreting, and using evidence about learning. An assignment or test is only one component.

## Begin with purpose

Possible purposes:
- diagnose prior knowledge;
- guide teaching;
- support student self-regulation;
- provide practice;
- certify achievement;
- select or rank;
- evaluate a course or program;
- assure professional competence.

A design suitable for one purpose may be invalid for another. State the decisions that results will support.

## Assessment triangle

A sound assessment aligns three vertices:

1. **Cognition:** a model of the knowledge, skill, or practice to be assessed.
2. **Observation:** tasks and situations that elicit relevant performance.
3. **Interpretation:** rules or models for inferring capability from performance.

Misalignment examples:
- cognition: argument quality; observation: recall quiz;
- observation: group product; interpretation: each member is competent;
- observation: AI-generated report; interpretation: independent writing ability;
- cognition: laboratory judgment; observation: decontextualized definitions.

## Evidence-centered design

Work through:
- **claim:** what should we infer?
- **evidence:** what features would support the claim?
- **task:** what situation will elicit those features?
- **interpretation:** how will quality be judged?
- **use:** what decision follows?

Do not begin by choosing a familiar format.

## Validity

Validity concerns the interpretation and use of scores or judgments. Gather evidence that:
- content represents the domain;
- cognitive processes match the intended construct;
- scoring reflects relevant performance;
- relationships with other measures make sense;
- consequences are acceptable;
- alternative explanations are reduced.

Threats:
- construct underrepresentation;
- construct-irrelevant difficulty;
- coaching to superficial item patterns;
- language demands unrelated to the goal;
- technology barriers;
- excessive speed;
- hidden criteria;
- unobserved assistance;
- score use beyond intended purpose.

## Reliability and dependability

Reliability is consistency sufficient for the decision. Improve through:
- adequate sampling;
- clear tasks and criteria;
- scorer calibration;
- multiple observations;
- structured scoring;
- moderation;
- stable administration;
- item review;
- statistical analysis when appropriate.

A perfectly reliable measure can be invalid. For high-stakes decisions, use multiple evidence sources.

## Fairness and accessibility

Fair assessment:
- provides appropriate access;
- minimizes irrelevant barriers;
- avoids biased content and stereotypes;
- offers accommodations without changing the target construct;
- communicates expectations;
- uses accessible technology and formats;
- reviews differential outcomes;
- provides due process and consistent policies.

Universal flexibility is not always appropriate: the permitted mode depends on what is being assessed.

## Formative and summative are uses

The same task can be formative if evidence changes next action and summative if it contributes to a final judgment. “Ungraded” is not sufficient to make assessment formative; it must create a feedback loop.

## Authentic assessment

Authentic tasks can improve alignment with complex outcomes by requiring performance under meaningful conditions. They may also introduce:
- uncontrolled assistance;
- high workload;
- scoring subjectivity;
- unequal access to resources;
- construct-irrelevant production demands.

Use milestones, process evidence, clear criteria, and moderation.

## Sampling

No assessment captures the whole domain. Build a blueprint across:
- outcomes;
- content;
- cognitive demand;
- context;
- item/task format;
- weighting;
- difficulty;
- independence;
- time.

Avoid overinterpreting a score based on a narrow sample.

## Assessment system

Use complementary evidence:
- selected response for efficient breadth;
- constructed response for explanation;
- performance tasks for integrated capability;
- oral examination for probing reasoning;
- portfolios for development across time;
- observations for practice;
- projects for synthesis;
- controlled tasks for independent assurance.

Do not force one task to serve every purpose.

## Transparency

Students need:
- purpose;
- target outcomes;
- allowed resources;
- process requirements;
- quality criteria;
- examples;
- deadlines and milestones;
- feedback/revision plan;
- academic-integrity and GenAI rules;
- accessibility arrangements;
- how grades are derived.

Transparency reduces ambiguity, not rigor.

## Assessment literacy questions

- What claim will this evidence support?
- What else could explain performance?
- Is the evidence sufficient?
- Is the scoring dependable?
- Are consequences fair?
- Can students act on the results?
- Is the burden proportional?
- Does AI access change the inference?
- What evidence is missing?
