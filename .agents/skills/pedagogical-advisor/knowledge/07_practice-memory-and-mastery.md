# Practice, Memory, and Mastery

## Retrieval practice

Retrieval is the act of producing knowledge from memory. It improves later access and provides diagnostic information.

Use:
- low-stakes quizzes;
- free recall;
- short-answer prompts;
- problem reconstruction;
- oral explanation;
- flashcards with complete retrieval;
- cumulative questions;
- blank-page summaries;
- diagram labeling from memory;
- explain-before-looking routines.

Provide feedback. Require retrieval of the intended form: recognition questions alone do not fully prepare free recall or production.

## Spacing

Distribute encounters with important material across time. The optimal gap depends on desired retention, but a practical course rule is:
- retrieve in the same session;
- retrieve in the next session;
- retrieve after one or more weeks;
- use cumulatively on later assessments;
- revisit in a new context.

Spacing works by requiring reconstruction after some forgetting and by supporting consolidation. Avoid a single “review week” as the only return.

## Interleaving and variation

Interleave when students must decide which method, category, or principle applies. Mix related problem types after students can initially perform each.

Benefits can include:
- discrimination;
- strategy selection;
- flexible retrieval;
- resistance to surface-feature matching.

Risks:
- overload before basic schemas exist;
- frustration without explanation;
- superficial mixing of unrelated tasks.

Use blocked examples → mixed comparison → independent interleaved practice.

## Generation and pretesting

Attempting a problem or prediction before instruction can prepare attention and improve later learning, even when the initial attempt is wrong. Use:
- prediction questions;
- “invent a rule” tasks;
- case diagnosis before explanation;
- attempted outline;
- estimate before calculation.

Keep search bounded and provide timely resolution. Do not leave high-confidence errors uncorrected.

## Self-explanation

Prompt:
- Why is this step valid?
- What principle is used?
- How does this relate to prior knowledge?
- Why does the alternative fail?
- What changes if this condition changes?
- Where did the example make a decision?

Self-explanation is especially useful with worked examples and representations.

## Desirable difficulties

A difficulty is desirable only if it causes productive processing and improves later performance. Useful difficulties include spacing, retrieval, variation, and generation. Undesirable difficulties include:
- ambiguous instructions;
- inaccessible formats;
- irrelevant complexity;
- excessive workload;
- missing prerequisites;
- delayed feedback that permits misconceptions to persist;
- punitive stakes.

Difficulty must be interpreted through learning, not discomfort.

## Deliberate and goal-directed practice

Effective practice:
- targets a specific capability;
- sits at an achievable challenge level;
- produces interpretable performance;
- includes feedback;
- allows correction and repetition;
- increases complexity;
- requires sufficient quantity;
- integrates components into whole performance.

“Practice makes permanent” is closer to reality than “practice makes perfect.” Repetition of error without diagnosis is harmful.

## Fluency and automaticity

Some components must become fast and accurate to free attention for higher-level work:
- symbol recognition;
- vocabulary;
- basic calculations;
- instrument routines;
- code syntax;
- factual anchors;
- procedural substeps.

Fluency practice should remain connected to meaning and conditions of use. Speed should follow accuracy and understanding.

## Cumulative course design

For each core concept:
- Week N: introduction and supported use.
- Week N+1: retrieval and a close application.
- Weeks N+2–4: mixed use with related concepts.
- Midcourse: integrative assessment.
- Later: novel transfer.
- End: cumulative demonstration.
- After assessment: error correction and re-use.

## Low-stakes quizzing

Useful conditions:
- predictable;
- cumulative;
- small grade weight or recovery;
- rapid feedback;
- no makeup bureaucracy for each item;
- multiple attempts or dropping a small number;
- questions aligned with meaningful outcomes;
- data used to guide teaching.

Quizzes should support learning, not create constant surveillance.

## Practice assignment design

A practice set should include:
1. prerequisite retrieval;
2. one or two modeled examples;
3. near practice;
4. mixed selection;
5. an explanation or error-analysis item;
6. a transfer item;
7. self-check and next-step guidance.

## Illusions of knowing

Fluency from rereading, highlighting, copying, and massed practice can feel like mastery. Counter with:
- closed-book retrieval;
- delayed checks;
- confidence ratings;
- prediction of performance;
- explanation;
- novel application;
- comparison of predicted and actual results.

## Evidence stance

- Retrieval practice: **A**.
- Distributed practice: **A**.
- Interleaving: **B**, strong in classification and problem discrimination, context-sensitive.
- Self-explanation: **B**.
- Generation/pretesting: **B**.
- Rereading/highlighting as primary strategies: **X/D**; they may support orientation but should not be the main learning method.
