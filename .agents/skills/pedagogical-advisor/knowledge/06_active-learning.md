# Active Learning

## Definition

Active learning means that students perform cognitive work relevant to the learning goal. Physical activity, group membership, clicking, attendance, and talking are not sufficient.

Examples of relevant work:
- retrieving;
- predicting;
- explaining;
- generating;
- comparing;
- classifying;
- choosing a strategy;
- constructing an argument;
- solving;
- critiquing;
- revising;
- reflecting with evidence.

The strongest undergraduate STEM synthesis in the project corpus found improved examination performance and lower failure under active learning, with benefits across disciplines and class sizes. Later work indicates that inclusive, high-intensity implementation can narrow achievement gaps. These averages do not make every activity effective.

## ICAP lens

Use the ICAP framework as a design heuristic:

- **Passive:** receiving without overt manipulation.
- **Active:** manipulating or selecting given information.
- **Constructive:** generating output beyond what was provided.
- **Interactive:** partners constructively build on one another’s reasoning.

The predicted hierarchy is generally Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive, but the task must remain aligned, guided, and cognitively manageable. Interaction that exchanges wrong answers or divides work without reasoning is not high-quality interactive learning.

## Core ingredients

Effective active learning commonly includes:
- a consequential question or task;
- individual thinking before social influence;
- a visible commitment or product;
- explanation, not answer exchange alone;
- structured comparison of reasoning;
- instructor access to student thinking;
- feedback or resolution;
- connection to the target model;
- accountability and inclusive participation.

## Peer instruction cycle

1. Pose a conceptually diagnostic question.
2. Students answer individually and record confidence/reasoning.
3. If responses are productively distributed, students discuss with a partner.
4. Students answer again.
5. Instructor samples explanations and addresses reasoning.
6. Provide a concise resolution.
7. Revisit the concept in a later question.

Do not use peer discussion when nearly everyone is already correct or when no one has a viable knowledge base; adjust instruction.

## Think–pair–share

A useful version:
- **Think:** write a claim or solution.
- **Pair:** compare and identify the key difference.
- **Share:** report reasoning, not merely an answer.
- **Synthesize:** instructor or class builds the disciplinary conclusion.

A weak version lacks a product, accountability, or synthesis.

## Worksheets and tutorials

Design questions to:
- elicit predictions;
- sequence conceptual steps;
- force distinctions;
- connect representations;
- expose common errors;
- culminate in transfer.

Provide prompts and checkpoints; do not turn worksheets into fill-in transcription.

## Case and problem learning

Cases work when students have enough knowledge and guidance to:
- identify relevant facts;
- generate and evaluate hypotheses;
- locate knowledge gaps;
- learn needed information;
- apply it;
- reflect.

Novices need scaffolding, resources, examples, and facilitator questions. Open-ended complexity without guidance can consume cognitive resources in search rather than learning.

## Group work design

Include:
- positive interdependence;
- individual accountability;
- equitable participation;
- explicit roles only when useful;
- a shared intellectual product;
- criteria for quality;
- mechanisms for conflict and nonparticipation;
- peer and instructor feedback;
- a reflection on process and learning.

Avoid grading a group product as evidence that every member achieved an individual outcome.

## Inclusive participation

Use:
- silent writing before discussion;
- randomized or rotating reporting;
- structured turn-taking;
- assigned skeptic/evidence-checker roles;
- anonymous response systems;
- multiple channels for contribution;
- norms for uncertainty and revision;
- sufficient wait time;
- careful monitoring of who speaks and whose ideas are taken up.

Do not assume group work is inherently equitable.

## Active learning in lectures

Replace some exposition, not all explanation. A useful pattern:
- explain just enough;
- ask students to apply or predict;
- inspect responses;
- target the next explanation;
- apply again.

This makes teaching adaptive.

## Feeling versus learning

Students may feel that fluent lectures produce more learning than effortful active sessions, even when performance evidence shows the opposite. Be transparent:
- explain the purpose of struggle;
- make routines predictable;
- keep stakes low;
- show students evidence of progress;
- avoid unnecessary confusion;
- distinguish productive difficulty from poor instruction.

## Common failure modes

- activity is unrelated to the outcome;
- task is too easy or too difficult;
- students lack prerequisite knowledge;
- no individual commitment;
- one student does the work;
- incorrect reasoning circulates without resolution;
- time is consumed by logistics;
- instructor does not use response data;
- activity is assessed differently from course goals;
- excessive activity fragments coherence;
- students cannot access the materials or participation mode.
