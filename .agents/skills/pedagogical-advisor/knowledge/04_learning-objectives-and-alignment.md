# Learning Objectives, Assessment Claims, and Alignment

## Objectives as capabilities

A useful objective specifies:
- the learner;
- an observable or inferable capability;
- important content or context;
- relevant conditions;
- an appropriate standard or quality, when needed.

Weak: “Understand regression.”

Stronger: “Given a study description and output, select and justify an appropriate regression model, diagnose violated assumptions, interpret coefficients and uncertainty, and communicate limitations to a nontechnical audience.”

The stronger objective reveals required knowledge, decisions, evidence, practice, and criteria.

## Do not let verbs substitute for thinking

Bloom-style verbs are prompts, not guarantees. “Analyze” can describe a trivial sorting task; “identify” can require expert perception. Specify:
- object of cognition;
- representation;
- complexity;
- novelty;
- independence;
- quality criteria;
- transfer distance.

## Outcome types

Distinguish:
- **declarative:** concepts, facts, principles;
- **procedural:** methods and routines;
- **conditional:** when and why to apply;
- **strategic:** planning and selection;
- **epistemic:** standards of evidence and justification;
- **social/professional:** communication, collaboration, ethical action;
- **metacognitive:** monitoring and regulation;
- **adaptive:** novel transfer and revision.

A balanced course may need several types, but each needs different evidence.

## Assessment claims

Convert each objective into a claim:

> Students who have achieved this outcome can ______ under ______ conditions, at ______ quality.

Then ask:
- What would they say, do, select, create, or revise?
- What features of performance are evidence?
- What alternative explanations could produce the same performance?
- What support is allowed?
- Does the task require independent capability, collaborative capability, or tool-mediated capability?

This prevents an assessment from measuring a convenient proxy.

## The alignment chain

For every major outcome, align:

**Outcome → evidence → assessment task → criteria → learning activities → practice → feedback → grading**

Common breaks:
- outcome emphasizes transfer; exam rewards recall;
- assignment requires synthesis; class practice is note copying;
- teamwork outcome; only group product graded;
- feedback identifies errors; no revision occurs;
- rubric values reasoning; points mostly reward formatting;
- course claims independent skill; AI assistance is unrestricted and invisible;
- participation grade rewards frequency rather than contribution to learning.

## Learning progressions

An objective at the end of a course needs intermediate performances. Define:
- starting conception;
- novice performance;
- developing performance;
- competent performance;
- transfer/adaptive performance.

Example for source evaluation:
1. distinguish source types;
2. identify author, purpose, and evidence;
3. compare reliability using explicit criteria;
4. reconcile conflicting sources;
5. construct and qualify an evidence-based claim;
6. transfer the method to unfamiliar domains.

## Criteria and standards

Criteria describe dimensions of quality. Standards describe levels. Good criteria:
- represent the construct;
- are teachable and observable;
- are few enough to use;
- distinguish substantive quality from presentation conventions;
- can guide feedback and self-assessment.

Avoid criteria that reward effort, attendance, polish, or compliance when the intended claim is disciplinary capability—unless those are explicit course outcomes.

## Constructive alignment with flexibility

Alignment is not rigid uniformity. Multiple activities or assessment forms can target the same construct. Flexibility is appropriate when:
- choices preserve the core capability;
- criteria remain comparable;
- modality is not part of the construct;
- access improves without reducing challenge.

Example: students may communicate a policy analysis as a report, briefing, or recorded presentation if the intended construct is evidence-based reasoning and audience adaptation. If formal academic writing is the construct, modality cannot be freely substituted.

## Backward design questions

1. What would count as meaningful success?
2. What evidence would make that judgment defensible?
3. What component capabilities are necessary?
4. What misconceptions or bottlenecks are likely?
5. What practice would build those components and integration?
6. How will students receive and use feedback?
7. How will the capability recur after delay?
8. What supports will fade?
9. What access choices preserve the construct?
10. How will we know whether the design worked?

## Alignment table

Use a table with columns:
- outcome;
- knowledge/skill components;
- assessment evidence;
- formative checks;
- practice opportunities;
- feedback/revision;
- summative weight;
- AI conditions;
- accessibility notes;
- transfer context.

## Quality test

An aligned design allows a student to answer:
- What am I trying to learn?
- What does quality look like?
- What practice will help?
- How will I know where I am?
- What can I do next?
- What help and tools are allowed?
- How will this connect to future tasks?
