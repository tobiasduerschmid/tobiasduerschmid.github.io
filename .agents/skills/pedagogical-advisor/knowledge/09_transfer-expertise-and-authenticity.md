# Transfer, Expertise, and Authentic Practice

## Expertise

Experts:
- possess large, organized knowledge structures;
- notice deep patterns;
- retrieve relevant knowledge conditionally;
- plan and monitor;
- chunk information;
- automate components;
- adapt representations;
- evaluate evidence using disciplinary standards.

Experts can omit steps when teaching because those steps are automated. Diagnose expert blind spots by decomposing real performance and observing novices.

## Transfer problem

Students often fail to use classroom learning in a new context. Transfer depends on:
- what was learned;
- how it was organized;
- similarity of deep structure;
- recognition of applicability;
- retrieval cues;
- motivation;
- practice selecting and adapting;
- metacognitive prompting.

Teaching a generic “critical thinking skill” without disciplinary knowledge and practice is unlikely to transfer broadly.

## Design for transfer

1. Teach the underlying principle explicitly.
2. Use multiple examples.
3. Contrast similar-looking problems requiring different strategies.
4. Contrast different-looking problems sharing a principle.
5. Ask students to articulate conditions of use.
6. Vary context, representation, and surface features.
7. Reduce prompts over time.
8. Include novel applications.
9. Ask students to explain similarities and differences.
10. Return after delay.

## Near and far transfer

- **Near:** new problem with similar structure and context.
- **Intermediate:** changed representation or context.
- **Far:** substantially different domain or time.

Do not jump directly from one example to far transfer. Build a gradient.

## Authenticity

Authentic tasks approximate meaningful disciplinary, professional, civic, or personal practice. Authenticity can involve:
- real audiences;
- realistic constraints;
- incomplete information;
- multiple defensible solutions;
- use of disciplinary tools;
- iteration and revision;
- ethical consequences;
- collaboration;
- communication across expertise levels.

Authenticity is not sufficient by itself. A real-world task can overwhelm novices or hide the target skill. Use scaffolds, role clarity, interim products, and explicit criteria.

## Cognitive apprenticeship

Design:
- modeling of expert process;
- coaching during performance;
- scaffolding;
- articulation of reasoning;
- reflection and comparison;
- exploration with fading support.

Make tacit decisions visible through think-alouds, annotated artifacts, and contrast between novice and expert approaches.

## Analogies

Use analogies by mapping:
- source elements;
- target elements;
- relations;
- limits.

Ask learners to generate and critique the mapping. Surface similarity can mislead; explain where the analogy breaks.

## Cases

A case sequence can support transfer:
- canonical case;
- near variant;
- contrasting case;
- ambiguous case;
- novel case requiring adaptation.

Require justification and identify which evidence changes the decision.

## Projects and capstones

A capstone should:
- integrate multiple outcomes;
- include milestones;
- provide formative evidence;
- require process documentation;
- include individual assurance where teamwork occurs;
- support revision;
- use criteria reflecting professional standards;
- include reflection on adaptation and limitations.

## Adaptive expertise

Routine expertise is efficient in familiar situations. Adaptive expertise balances efficiency with innovation. Build it by:
- establishing sound routines;
- varying conditions;
- asking “why” and “what if”;
- comparing methods;
- diagnosing failure;
- rewarding justified adaptation;
- exposing uncertainty.

Do not ask novices to innovate before they possess resources to reason.

## Assessment of transfer

Use tasks that:
- alter surface features;
- require strategy selection;
- remove familiar cues;
- introduce irrelevant information;
- require explanation;
- ask for adaptation;
- delay testing.

Score both outcome and reasoning. A correct answer with an inappropriate method may not show transferable understanding.
