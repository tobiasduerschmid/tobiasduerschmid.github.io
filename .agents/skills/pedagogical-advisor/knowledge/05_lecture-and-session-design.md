# Lecture and Class-Session Design

A lecture is not inherently ineffective. The design problem is prolonged one-way transmission without sufficient processing, diagnosis, practice, or feedback. Effective sessions combine clear explanation with learner activity that serves a defined learning function.

## Session design frame

A session should have:
1. **Purpose:** the capability advanced today.
2. **Connection:** retrieval of prior learning and placement in the course map.
3. **Question/problem:** a reason to learn the new material.
4. **Explanation/modeling:** concise, structured, signaled instruction.
5. **Guided processing:** predictions, examples, self-explanation, comparison.
6. **Practice:** students perform the target thinking.
7. **Feedback:** peers, instructor, model answers, or automated checks.
8. **Synthesis:** students reconstruct the main model or decision rule.
9. **Bridge:** preparation for delayed retrieval and next use.

## Design from a bottleneck

Do not begin with “What slides should I make?” Begin with:
- What is difficult for novices?
- What do experts notice or decide automatically?
- What misconception will distort the explanation?
- Which representation is essential?
- Which step needs modeling?
- What must students try during the session?

A session can advance one or two substantial ideas more effectively than cover many disconnected points.

## Explain clearly

Clear explanations:
- identify the organizing question;
- state a map before details;
- signal transitions and causal relationships;
- distinguish principle from example;
- use examples and nonexamples;
- make expert decisions visible;
- state conditions and limitations;
- periodically reconstruct the whole.

Avoid:
- reading slides;
- unexplained notation;
- simultaneous dense text and speech;
- decorative images that compete for attention;
- examples without explicit mapping to principles;
- assuming that a completed demonstration produced understanding.

## Segment by cognitive function, not a mythical timer

Change activity when the learning function changes:
- orient;
- explain;
- model;
- predict;
- practice;
- compare;
- debate;
- retrieve;
- synthesize.

A ten-minute segment can be useful operationally, but there is no universal scientific rule that attention ends at a fixed minute. Monitor evidence of processing.

## Questioning architecture

Use questions for distinct purposes:
- **diagnostic:** What do students currently think?
- **prediction:** What will happen and why?
- **discrimination:** Which principle applies?
- **explanation:** Why is this answer justified?
- **comparison:** How do these cases differ?
- **transfer:** What changes in a new context?
- **metacognitive:** What made this difficult?
- **decision:** What evidence would change your judgment?

Build wait time, individual commitment, peer discussion, and whole-class synthesis. “Any questions?” is a weak diagnostic because students may not know what they misunderstand.

## Demonstrations

Before a demonstration:
1. ask for prediction and reasoning;
2. collect commitments;
3. run the demonstration;
4. compare outcomes;
5. explain discrepancies;
6. retrieve the principle later.

A demonstration watched passively can entertain without changing the learner’s model.

## Examples

For novice procedural learning:
- show a fully worked example;
- annotate goals and decisions;
- ask self-explanation questions;
- compare correct and flawed examples;
- move to completion problems;
- fade steps;
- then require independent solution and mixed selection.

For conceptual learning:
- use contrasting cases;
- vary surface features while holding principle constant;
- vary principle while holding surface features similar;
- ask students to generate examples and counterexamples.

## Note-taking support

Do not require students to decide structure while simultaneously decoding high-density new content. Provide:
- partial outlines;
- diagrams with missing labels;
- guiding questions;
- space for examples and connections;
- pauses for reconstruction.

Avoid complete notes that eliminate processing. The goal is generative organization, not transcription.

## A 50-minute default

- 0–5: retrieval/prediction and purpose
- 5–15: explanation/model
- 15–23: individual attempt
- 23–30: peer comparison and justification
- 30–38: instructor diagnosis and targeted explanation
- 38–45: second application or transfer
- 45–50: retrieval summary, exit evidence, bridge

This is a starting pattern, not a required schedule.

## Large lectures

Use:
- response systems;
- colored cards or hand signals;
- think–pair–share with explicit prompts;
- peer instruction;
- brief worksheets;
- prediction-observation cycles;
- minute papers;
- error analysis;
- structured question collection;
- sampled explanations from students.

Individual accountability should precede peer discussion. The instructor must use responses to decide what happens next.

## Seminars and discussions

Design around:
- a contestable question;
- evidence-rich preparation;
- distributed participation;
- protocols for interpretation and critique;
- synthesis of agreements, disagreements, and unresolved issues;
- a product such as a claim map, analytic memo, or revised interpretation.

Discussion is not automatically learning. It needs intellectual purpose and evidence of changed understanding.

## Closing the session

A strong close asks students to:
- retrieve the main model without looking;
- explain the hardest distinction;
- solve a short transfer item;
- identify a remaining question;
- connect the session to prior and next learning.

Use exit evidence to revise the next session. Do not merely restate the agenda.

## Session quality questions

- Did students perform the target thinking?
- Was prior knowledge activated?
- Did explanation reduce unnecessary load?
- Were examples processed rather than displayed?
- Was there individual accountability?
- Did feedback occur before the session ended?
- Did the instructor respond to evidence?
- Is there a delayed retrieval plan?
- Can all students access the materials and participate?
- Is any AI use purposeful and explicit?
