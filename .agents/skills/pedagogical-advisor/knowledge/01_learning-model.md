# Integrated Learning Model

Use this model to diagnose why a design may or may not produce durable, usable learning.

## 1. Learning is a change in capability, not exposure

Teaching inputs—readings, lectures, videos, activities, feedback—are opportunities. Learning is an enduring change in what a learner can retrieve, understand, perform, judge, create, or transfer. A polished product produced with extensive help may not demonstrate independent capability; a correct answer may reflect guessing or imitation; engagement may coexist with weak learning.

Design therefore needs:
- a precise capability target;
- cognitive and social work likely to build it;
- practice distributed over time;
- evidence that distinguishes performance support from learning.

## 2. Prior knowledge is the entry system

Learners interpret new information through existing knowledge, beliefs, experiences, language, values, and strategies. Prior knowledge may be:
- accurate or inaccurate;
- sufficient or insufficient;
- activated or inert;
- appropriate or misapplied.

Consequences:
- Diagnose consequential prerequisites and misconceptions.
- Activate relevant knowledge before explanation.
- Use contrasting cases, prediction, and elicitation to expose mental models.
- Do not mistake silence for absence of ideas.
- Correct misconceptions through explanation, evidence, comparison, and repeated use; simply stating the right answer is often insufficient.

## 3. Attention is selective and limited

Learners cannot process everything in a presentation. Attention is shaped by goals, salience, prior knowledge, emotion, expectations, and competing demands. Design attention rather than demanding it:
- signal structure and priorities;
- remove decorative competition;
- use questions and predictions to orient attention;
- segment long explanations;
- alternate reception with purposeful cognitive activity;
- make transitions and connections explicit.

There is no universal short classroom attention-span timer. The relevant question is whether the task continues to require meaningful processing and whether learners can identify what matters.

## 4. Working memory constrains new learning

Novel, interacting information can exceed working-memory capacity. Long-term knowledge reduces this burden through schemas and fluent procedures. Instruction should manage load by:
- decomposing without destroying conceptual coherence;
- integrating mutually dependent sources;
- using worked examples for novices;
- signaling essential relationships;
- pretraining components when necessary;
- avoiding redundant text, narration, and visuals;
- fading support as schemas develop.

The same support can become redundant for experts. Expertise is a design variable, not a demographic label.

## 5. Learners construct organized knowledge

Useful knowledge is not a list of facts. Experts organize knowledge around deep principles, relationships, conditions of application, and disciplinary practices. Students may organize around surface features or course chronology.

Design for organization:
- use concept maps, comparison tables, causal diagrams, timelines, models, and taxonomies;
- make big ideas and relationships visible;
- ask students to classify, explain, contrast, and connect;
- revisit the same concept across cases and representational forms;
- assess when and why a concept applies, not only its definition.

## 6. Retrieval changes memory

Attempting to recall knowledge strengthens later access and reveals gaps. Retrieval is most useful when:
- it is effortful but possible;
- feedback follows;
- questions are cumulative;
- formats require the intended knowledge, not recognition alone;
- the stakes are low enough to support honest practice;
- success is not confused with permanent mastery.

Retrieval can target facts, concepts, procedures, explanations, decisions, and whole performance routines.

## 7. Spacing and variation build durability and discrimination

Practice massed into one session produces fluency that can be mistaken for learning. Spacing allows some forgetting and requires reconstruction. Interleaving and variation can improve the ability to discriminate problem types and select strategies.

Use:
- brief cumulative questions across sessions;
- delayed opportunities to use earlier ideas;
- mixed problem sets after initial acquisition;
- varied contexts and representations;
- spiral return with increased complexity.

Do not interleave everything immediately. Initial blocked practice can help novices establish a method before mixed selection is required.

## 8. Generation and explanation deepen processing

Learners benefit from producing beyond what is given:
- prediction before observation;
- attempted solution before instruction;
- self-explanation of steps and principles;
- explanation to a peer;
- drawing or modeling relationships;
- generating examples, counterexamples, or questions.

Generation should be constrained enough to avoid unproductive search, especially for novices. Error is useful when feedback and correction follow; unmanaged error can consolidate misconceptions.

## 9. Feedback closes a control loop

Effective feedback supplies information the learner or teacher can use to reduce a gap between current and desired performance. Its value depends on:
- a clear goal and success criteria;
- diagnostic specificity;
- timing before the next meaningful attempt;
- learner capacity to interpret and act;
- opportunities for revision or new practice.

Feedback can operate at:
- task level: correctness and missing elements;
- process level: strategy and reasoning;
- self-regulation level: planning, monitoring, help seeking;
- self level: global praise or blame, usually least informative.

Feedback to the teacher—evidence about what students understood—can be more consequential than comments sent to students after a unit is over.

## 10. Mastery requires components, integration, and fluency

Complex performance involves:
1. component knowledge and skills;
2. knowing when and why to use them;
3. integrating components;
4. increasing fluency;
5. adapting to variation.

A course can fail by:
- assuming prerequisites;
- practicing components without integration;
- assigning whole tasks without scaffolding;
- rewarding speed before accuracy and understanding;
- never removing scaffolds;
- not providing enough repetitions across time.

## 11. Motivation regulates engagement and persistence

Motivation is not a fixed trait. Learners are more likely to invest when they:
- value the goal or can connect it to meaningful purposes;
- expect that effort and strategy can lead to success;
- experience achievable challenge and credible support;
- have meaningful choices within clear structure;
- perceive belonging, fairness, and respect;
- can interpret errors as information rather than identity verdicts.

Motivational messages cannot compensate for incoherent instruction, inaccessible tasks, or repeated failure.

## 12. Metacognition guides self-directed learning

Self-regulated learners:
- assess task demands;
- evaluate strengths and gaps;
- choose strategies;
- allocate time and resources;
- monitor progress;
- adjust;
- evaluate outcomes.

These skills should be taught, modeled, prompted, and practiced. Reflection without evidence can become storytelling; pair reflection with performance data, predictions, error analysis, and revised action.

## 13. Learning is social, cultural, and situated

Participation structures affect who speaks, whose knowledge counts, what errors mean, and what identities feel compatible with the discipline. Culture influences interpretation, interaction, help seeking, relevance, and assessment.

Design implications:
- learn about students without stereotyping;
- use multiple examples and contexts;
- make tacit disciplinary norms explicit;
- distribute authority and participation;
- establish norms for disagreement and error;
- distinguish barriers from deficits;
- connect classroom learning to communities and professional practice.

## 14. Transfer must be engineered

Transfer is more likely when learners:
- understand underlying principles;
- encounter varied examples and contexts;
- compare cases;
- know conditions of applicability;
- practice selecting strategies;
- receive prompts that gradually disappear;
- reflect on similarities and differences;
- use knowledge in authentic or novel situations.

“Students learned it once” is not a transfer plan.

## 15. Consolidation takes time

Sleep, time, repeated retrieval, and subsequent integration contribute to stabilization and reorganization. Course design should not expect one exposure to support long-term capability. Important ideas should recur across days, weeks, and assessments.

## Diagnostic use

When students underperform, test competing explanations:

- **Knowledge:** missing prerequisite, misconception, weak organization?
- **Load:** too much novelty, split attention, redundant media?
- **Practice:** too little, too massed, too uniform, too supported?
- **Feedback:** absent, late, vague, unusable?
- **Motivation:** low value, low expectancy, threat, weak belonging?
- **Metacognition:** poor calibration, ineffective strategy, weak planning?
- **Assessment:** task does not sample the taught capability, or taught work does not prepare for the task?
- **Environment:** access, language, technology, time, climate, or inequitable participation?
- **Transfer:** insufficient variation or explicit abstraction?

Do not choose a single explanation before examining evidence.
