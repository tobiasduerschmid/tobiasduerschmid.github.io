# Formative Assessment and Feedback

## Formative assessment is a decision process

A formative loop:
1. clarify the target;
2. elicit evidence;
3. interpret the evidence;
4. decide the next action;
5. act;
6. elicit new evidence.

Evidence can change:
- instructor explanation or pacing;
- grouping;
- practice tasks;
- student strategy;
- help seeking;
- revision;
- resource allocation.

If nothing changes, the activity was only a check.

## Useful formative methods

- prerequisite quiz;
- concept inventory;
- prediction question;
- hinge question;
- one-minute explanation;
- error analysis;
- worked-example completion;
- confidence rating;
- think–pair–share product;
- exit ticket;
- draft or prototype;
- oral probing;
- annotated concept map;
- retrieval quiz;
- muddiest-point response;
- self-assessment against criteria.

Choose the method based on the decision needed.

## Hinge questions

A hinge question occurs at a decision point and:
- targets a critical concept;
- includes distractors based on misconceptions;
- can be answered quickly;
- yields interpretable response patterns;
- determines whether to move, reteach, or differentiate.

A vague “Do you understand?” cannot serve this function.

## Feedback model

Effective feedback answers:
- Where am I going?
- Where am I now?
- What is the next productive action?

It should be:
- tied to goals and criteria;
- specific enough to guide action;
- focused on high-leverage issues;
- timed for use;
- proportionate;
- credible;
- followed by revision or new practice.

## Feedback levels

- **Task:** correct/incorrect, missing evidence, local features.
- **Process:** strategy, reasoning, approach, representation.
- **Self-regulation:** planning, monitoring, checking, help seeking.
- **Self:** general praise or criticism.

Use self-level praise sparingly. Praise strategies or effective decisions without implying that praise itself teaches the next step.

## Timing

Immediate feedback is useful when:
- errors can consolidate;
- the task is procedural;
- learners are novices;
- safety matters.

Delayed feedback can support:
- independent monitoring;
- complex writing;
- retrieval before correction;
- reflection.

The key is availability before the next consequential attempt.

## Amount

More comments can reduce use. Prioritize:
- one or two global issues;
- patterns, not every local error;
- next actions;
- examples or questions;
- opportunities to apply.

Separate editing from conceptual feedback.

## Revision

Feedback without revision often has weak impact. Require students to:
- submit a revision memo;
- highlight changes;
- explain which feedback they accepted or rejected;
- reattempt similar problems;
- correct an exam and justify changes;
- compare original and revised reasoning.

Grade some evidence of feedback use, not blind compliance.

## Peer feedback

Peer assessment can improve evaluative judgment when:
- criteria are explicit;
- students analyze exemplars first;
- feedback is structured;
- identities and power are considered;
- students receive multiple perspectives;
- the author decides how to revise;
- the instructor monitors quality.

Untrained peer feedback can be inaccurate. Use it for learning and comparison, not as the sole high-stakes judgment.

## Self-assessment and calibration

Ask students to:
- predict a score;
- judge work against criteria;
- identify evidence;
- compare with external feedback;
- explain discrepancies;
- plan a change.

Self-assessment is a capability to develop, not an accurate measure by default.

## Exemplars

Use multiple examples:
- strong and weak;
- different successful approaches;
- annotated features;
- borderline cases.

Ask students to apply criteria before revealing expert analysis. One “perfect” example can encourage imitation rather than judgment.

## Feedback to the teacher

Collect:
- response distributions;
- common errors;
- question patterns;
- notes and representations;
- work samples;
- time-on-task;
- help requests.

Change the next teaching move. This feedback loop is central to responsive teaching.

## Exam wrappers

After an exam, students:
- classify errors;
- compare preparation to demands;
- evaluate predictions;
- identify strategy changes;
- set a plan for cumulative learning.

Use later follow-up to check whether plans changed. A reflection completed and forgotten is not self-regulation.

## Grading and formative use

High stakes reduce willingness to expose uncertainty. Keep early attempts low stakes, permit recovery, and separate practice from certification where possible. Small accountability can improve completion without turning every error into a penalty.

## Evidence stance

Feedback overall has meaningful but heterogeneous effects: **B**.
Goal-linked, actionable feedback with opportunity to use it: **A/B**.
Peer/self-assessment with training and criteria: **B**.
“Give more comments”: **X** as a general rule.
