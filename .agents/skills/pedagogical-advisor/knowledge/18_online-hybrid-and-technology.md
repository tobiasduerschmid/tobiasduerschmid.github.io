# Online, Hybrid, and Learning Technology

## Choose technology by learning function

Ask:
- What cognitive or social process is needed?
- What does technology enable better?
- What new barriers or data risks arise?
- What happens if the tool fails?
- Is the benefit worth setup and support?

Technology is not pedagogy.

## Online learning cycle

Design:
1. orientation and purpose;
2. activation/retrieval;
3. concise explanation or resource;
4. guided practice;
5. interaction;
6. feedback;
7. revision;
8. synthesis;
9. delayed return.

Avoid repositories of long videos and readings followed by a high-stakes test.

## Video

Use:
- short, coherent segments;
- signaling;
- captions and transcripts;
- learner control;
- embedded retrieval or prediction;
- stable companion notes;
- explicit follow-up tasks.

Video length should follow conceptual units, not a universal number.

## Asynchronous interaction

Good prompts require interdependence:
- compare two interpretations;
- improve a peer’s model;
- challenge evidence;
- jointly solve a case;
- synthesize a group position.

Set deadlines that permit response and uptake. Instructor presence includes synthesis, not replying to every post.

## Synchronous sessions

Reserve for:
- diagnosis;
- discussion;
- practice;
- simulation;
- coaching;
- feedback;
- community.

Do not use scarce live time to repeat material that could be accessed flexibly.

## Automated feedback

Useful for:
- frequent practice;
- immediate correctness;
- hints;
- adaptive sequencing;
- basic code or calculation checks.

Risks:
- false positives/negatives;
- gaming;
- overfocus on measurable components;
- weak explanation;
- accessibility;
- privacy.

Combine with human judgment for complex outcomes.

## Learning analytics

Use analytics as signals, not labels. Clicks and time-on-page are weak proxies for learning. Establish:
- legitimate purpose;
- minimal data collection;
- transparency;
- privacy;
- validation;
- human review;
- no punitive use from ambiguous indicators.

## Hybrid coherence

Ensure online and in-person components form one sequence:
- online preparation is used in class;
- class generates evidence or products used online;
- tasks are not duplicates;
- students understand the purpose of each mode.

## Access

Provide:
- low-bandwidth alternatives;
- mobile-compatible materials;
- downloadable resources;
- captions/transcripts;
- flexible windows;
- accessible documents;
- technology support;
- clear time-zone and outage policies.

## Community

Create:
- instructor welcome and course map;
- predictable announcements;
- small groups;
- student profiles within privacy limits;
- early low-stakes interaction;
- help channels;
- norms;
- feedback presence.

## Evidence stance

Online learning can be effective when instructional design, interaction, practice, and support are strong. Mode alone is not the causal factor. Blended designs may benefit from added time and redesign; do not attribute all gains to technology.
