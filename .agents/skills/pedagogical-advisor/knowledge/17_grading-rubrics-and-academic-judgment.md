# Grading, Rubrics, and Academic Judgment

## Separate functions

Grades may communicate achievement, motivate completion, certify standards, and make decisions. Combining every behavior into one score can obscure learning.

Distinguish:
- achievement;
- practice;
- participation;
- timeliness;
- collaboration;
- professionalism;
- improvement.

Include non-achievement factors only when they are explicit outcomes or necessary conditions, and label them.

## Criterion-referenced judgment

Compare work with defined standards, not the cohort. Norm referencing may be appropriate for selection, but it is usually weak for communicating what a student can do.

## Rubrics

A rubric includes:
- criteria;
- levels or standards;
- descriptors;
- scoring strategy.

### Analytic
Scores criteria separately. Useful for diagnostic feedback and complex tasks. Risks: fragmentation and false precision.

### Holistic
Makes an integrated judgment. Useful when quality is emergent or time is limited. Risks: less diagnostic transparency.

### Single-point
States the expected standard with space for evidence above and below. Useful for feedback; less useful for highly standardized scoring.

## Good criteria

Criteria should:
- reflect the learning construct;
- be distinct enough to guide judgment;
- use disciplinary language that is taught;
- focus on quality, not task directions;
- avoid counting superficial features;
- be few enough to apply reliably.

## Descriptors

Describe observable qualities, not adjectives alone.

Weak: “Excellent analysis.”

Stronger: “Selects relevant evidence, explains how it supports the claim, addresses plausible counterevidence, and qualifies conclusions according to uncertainty.”

Avoid making every cell unique prose if the distinctions are not real.

## Rubric development

1. Define the construct.
2. Collect examples.
3. Identify quality dimensions.
4. Draft the proficient standard.
5. Describe meaningful variation.
6. Test on sample work.
7. revise overlaps and gaps.
8. calibrate scorers.
9. share with students and practice applying.
10. review after use.

## Exemplars and evaluative judgment

Students learn standards by comparing work, applying criteria, discussing disagreements, and revising. A rubric alone may not create understanding.

Use anonymized/composite examples and multiple successful approaches.

## Calibration and moderation

For significant assessments:
- score sample scripts;
- discuss criteria;
- document decisions;
- use anchor responses;
- rescore periodically;
- review borderline cases;
- examine subgroup patterns.

Reliability is a design responsibility, not proof that judgment should be eliminated.

## Grading architecture

Prefer:
- low stakes for practice;
- sufficient weight for major outcomes;
- cumulative evidence;
- recovery after feedback;
- transparent weighting;
- no single fragile measure dominating;
- policies that avoid mathematical artifacts.

Avoid:
- points for everything;
- averaging early novice attempts with later mastery without reason;
- grade penalties disproportionate to learning;
- extra credit unrelated to outcomes;
- participation grades based on instructor impression alone.

## Specifications and mastery grading

Specifications grading can clarify binary standards and allow revision. It requires:
- well-designed specifications;
- meaningful task bundles;
- manageable reassessment;
- quality control;
- communication.

Mastery approaches should not reduce complex quality to arbitrary checklists.

## Feedback and grades

Grades can crowd out feedback. Sequence:
- feedback first;
- reflection or revision;
- grade later, when feasible.

For final work, include feedforward to future tasks.

## AI-assisted grading

AI may help draft comments, cluster errors, or check consistency, but instructors remain responsible for:
- privacy;
- accuracy;
- bias;
- criteria;
- final judgment;
- explainability;
- institutional policy.

Do not upload protected student work to unauthorized systems. Do not let model fluency replace evidence.
