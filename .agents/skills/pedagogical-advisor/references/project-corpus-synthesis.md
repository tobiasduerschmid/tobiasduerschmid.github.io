# Project Corpus Synthesis

This overview maps the sixteen project sources to teaching decisions. The 2026-09-05 passage-level update in `references/project-foundations-2026.md` and `references/project-extended-2026.md` gives verified identities, exact attachment page locators, inspection limits, and operational recipes. Those inspected-source records take precedence over this broad orientation. This is a synthesis, not a substitute for the originals.

## 1. *How People Learn: Brain, Mind, Experience, and School* — National Research Council

**Central contributions**
- Expertise depends on organized, conditional knowledge—not generic thinking skills alone.
- Prior knowledge shapes new learning and can help or impede it.
- Transfer requires understanding, varied use, and attention to contexts of applicability.
- Effective learning environments align learner-centered, knowledge-centered, assessment-centered, and community-centered perspectives.
- Formative assessment and metacognition are integral to learning.

**Used in this skill**
- learning model; course design; prior-knowledge diagnosis; transfer; learning-environment alignment; formative assessment.

**Boundary**
- Broad consensus synthesis rather than a recipe. Many examples are K–12 and need adaptation to higher education and specific disciplines.

## 2. *How We Learn: The New Science of Education and the Brain* — Stanislas Dehaene

**Central contributions**
- Four broad pillars: attention, active engagement, error feedback, and consolidation.
- Learning changes neural systems through experience and practice.
- Attention is selective; errors become useful when informative feedback supports model revision.
- Sleep, repeated activation, and consolidation matter for durable learning.

**Used in this skill**
- lecture design; feedback; retrieval/spacing; explanations of attention and consolidation.

**Boundary**
- Neuroscience supplies mechanisms and constraints but rarely determines a classroom method by itself. Avoid “brain-based” overtranslation.

## 3. Freeman et al. (2014), *Active learning increases student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics*

**Central contributions**
- Meta-analysis of 225 undergraduate STEM studies found better examination/concept-inventory performance under active learning and higher failure risk under traditional lecture.
- Benefits appeared across disciplines and class sizes, with variation by assessment and implementation.

**Used in this skill**
- default preference for structured active learning in STEM; large-class orchestration; critique of lecture-only control conditions.

**Boundary**
- “Active learning” is a broad family of implementations. The result does not imply that every group activity is effective or that explanation should disappear.

## 4. *How Learning Works: Seven Research-Based Principles for Smart Teaching* — Ambrose et al.

**Central contributions**
- Seven connected principles: prior knowledge; knowledge organization; motivation; mastery; goal-directed practice and feedback; development and climate; self-directed learning.
- Practical translation of learning science for college teaching.
- Useful tools include objectives, concept maps, rubrics, exam wrappers, checklists, and peer review.

**Used in this skill**
- operating model; learner diagnosis; alignment; practice-feedback cycles; climate; self-regulation; templates.

**Boundary**
- Principles are intentionally general; local disciplinary knowledge and implementation evidence remain necessary.

## 5. *Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning* — Brown, Roediger, and McDaniel

**Central contributions**
- Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, variation, generation, elaboration, and calibration support durable learning.
- Rereading, massed practice, and subjective fluency can create illusions of knowing.
- Desirable difficulties should be learning-relevant and paired with feedback.

**Used in this skill**
- cumulative quizzes; homework architecture; practice scheduling; exam wrappers; distinction between productive and arbitrary difficulty.

**Boundary**
- A research-informed trade book; use the cited primary literature for precise effect estimates and boundary conditions.

## 6. *Visible Learning* — John Hattie

**Central contributions**
- Large synthesis organized around making learning visible to teachers and teaching visible to learners.
- Emphasizes challenging goals, success criteria, feedback, teacher responsiveness, and evaluation of impact.
- Warns that “everything works” if the comparison point is too weak.

**Used in this skill**
- goals/success criteria; feedback-to-teacher loop; evaluation mindset; critique of unexamined innovation.

**Boundary**
- Rankings of average effect sizes should not be treated as a universal shopping list. Meta-meta-analysis mixes constructs, designs, outcomes, and contexts; moderators and study quality matter.

## 7. *Tools for Teaching* — Barbara Gross Davis

**Central contributions**
- Practical compendium spanning course preparation, discussion, lectures, collaborative learning, motivation, writing, homework, testing, grading, technology, and fast feedback.
- Strong emphasis on adapting techniques to context and evaluating their effects.

**Used in this skill**
- operational checklists, discussion and lecture tactics, fast feedback, course-material review, workload-aware implementation.

**Boundary**
- Published in 1993; technology, accessibility, and some psychological claims require updating. The book's historical discussion of learning styles is not accepted as a basis for matching instruction.

## 8. Dunlosky et al. (2013), *Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques*

**Central contributions**
- Systematic review of ten learner techniques across conditions, learners, materials, and criterion tasks.
- Practice testing and distributed practice received high-utility judgments; interleaving, self-explanation, and elaborative interrogation were promising/conditional; highlighting and rereading were low utility as primary strategies.

**Used in this skill**
- evidence levels for study/practice techniques; design of homework and learner guidance; myth correction.

**Boundary**
- Utility judgments reflect evidence available in 2013 and broad generalizability, not the impossibility of useful niche applications.

## 9. *Cognitive Load Theory* — Plass, Moreno, and Brünken (eds.)

**Central contributions**
- Instruction should respect limited working memory and support schema construction.
- Worked examples, integrated representations, reduction of redundancy/split attention, and guidance can aid novices.
- Expertise reversal means supports beneficial to novices may become redundant or harmful to advanced learners.

**Used in this skill**
- multimedia and lecture design; worked examples; scaffolding and fading; expertise adaptation; task decomposition.

**Boundary**
- Cognitive-load measurement is difficult, and terminology has evolved. Do not equate all difficulty with harmful load or use CLT to justify passive teaching.

## 10. *Handbook of Competence and Motivation: Theory and Application* — Elliot, Dweck, and Yeager (eds.)

**Central contributions**
- Integrates expectancy/value, achievement goals, attribution, self-efficacy, interest, self-determination, belonging, emotion, anxiety, and self-regulation.
- Motivation is situated, developmental, social, and responsive to learning environments.
- Utility-value and targeted psychological interventions can help under conditions, especially when well timed and contextually supported.

**Used in this skill**
- motivation diagnosis; value; autonomy within structure; belonging; self-efficacy; climate and targeted interventions.

**Boundary**
- Small psychological interventions are not magic and can be context-dependent. Structural barriers cannot be repaired by messaging alone.

## 11. *In Search of Understanding: The Case for Constructivist Classrooms* — Brooks and Brooks

**Central contributions**
- Treat learners as thinkers with existing theories.
- Seek student perspectives, challenge suppositions, organize around big ideas, use relevant problems, and embed assessment in teaching.
- Values inquiry, primary data, and meaning making over reproduction.

**Used in this skill**
- learner-centered diagnosis; conceptual change; authentic problems; discussion and inquiry; embedded formative assessment.

**Boundary**
- Constructivism is a theory of knowledge/learning, not a warrant for minimal guidance. Novices often need explicit models, explanations, and scaffolds.

## 12. *Generative AI in Computer Science Education: Challenges and Opportunities* — Franklin et al. (2025)

**Central contributions**
- Reviews rapidly evolving GenAI capabilities, instructor/student perceptions, preparation and instruction uses, misuse risks, emerging pedagogy, and a course case study.
- Warns about overreliance, shallow understanding, illusions of competence, and unsustainable “AI-proofing.”
- Highlights opportunities for feedback, resource generation, ambitious projects, and revised professional capabilities.

**Used in this skill**
- AI use matrices; process and independent evidence; verification; assessment redesign; learning-preserving guardrails.

**Boundary**
- Computing-focused and current only through its literature-search period. Model capability and policy change rapidly; update before current claims.

## 13. *Educational Psychology* — Anita Woolfolk

**Central contributions**
- Comprehensive integration of development, learning, motivation, diversity, classroom environments, teaching, and assessment.
- Practical guidance on rubrics, formative/summative uses, reliability, validity, bias, authentic assessment, inclusion, and differentiation.

**Used in this skill**
- assessment fundamentals; rubric design; inclusive teaching; motivational and developmental adaptation; educational-research literacy.

**Boundary**
- Broad textbook synthesis; specific practices should be checked against primary/current reviews. Some policy examples are edition-specific and outdated.

## 14. *What the Best College Teachers Do* — Ken Bain

**Central contributions**
- Exemplary teachers design “natural critical learning environments” around authentic, consequential questions.
- They focus on sustained, substantial learning, high expectations with support, student self-assessment, and evidence of intellectual development.
- Preparation begins with questions about learning rather than delivery.

**Used in this skill**
- brainstorming; authentic tasks; course purpose; critical learning environments; student agency and evaluative judgment.

**Boundary**
- Qualitative study of selected exemplary teachers; valuable for design hypotheses and conceptualization, not population effect estimates.

## 15. *The Cambridge Handbook of the Learning Sciences* — R. Keith Sawyer (ed.)

**Central contributions**
- Learning sciences bridges cognitive, social, cultural, disciplinary, technological, and design perspectives.
- Covers scaffolding, metacognition, conceptual change, cognitive apprenticeship, assessment, PBL/project learning, collaboration, argumentation, technology, disciplinary learning, equity, and design-based research.
- Assessment is modeled through cognition, observation, and interpretation; learning progressions connect targets, levels, performances, and evidence.

**Used in this skill**
- assessment triangle; learning progressions; PBL and collaboration; cognitive apprenticeship; authentic disciplinary practice; technology and design-based improvement.

**Boundary**
- A multi-author handbook contains diverse theoretical commitments. Practices must be interpreted through the quality and conditions of their specific evidence.

## 16. *The Mind within the Net* — Manfred Spitzer

Historical neural-network models illuminate the distinction between representations, associations, and learning. Use as conceptual background for discussing models and expertise; do not infer classroom treatment effects, diagnose learners, or prescribe teaching from neural analogies. ## Cross-corpus synthesis

The strongest shared design logic is:

1. begin with what learners know and how expertise in the domain is organized;
2. define consequential, observable capabilities;
3. model and explain selectively, especially for novices;
4. require learners to retrieve, generate, explain, compare, decide, practise, and reflect;
5. distribute and vary practice across time;
6. make reasoning and learning visible through aligned formative evidence;
7. provide feedback that can be used in another attempt;
8. build motivation through value, attainable challenge, agency, belonging, and credible support;
9. reduce irrelevant barriers while maintaining standards;
10. assess through defensible claim–observation–interpretation relationships;
11. adapt guidance to expertise and context;
12. evaluate implementation and learning rather than adopting labels or fads;
13. treat technology, including GenAI, as part of the learning system rather than a pedagogical cause by itself.

## Productive tensions retained in the skill

- **Explanation versus construction:** learners construct understanding, but novice construction is often best supported by explicit modeling and guidance.
- **Difficulty versus accessibility:** effort can improve learning, but irrelevant barriers and overload do not.
- **Authenticity versus reliability:** rich tasks improve relevance and transfer evidence but require careful sampling, criteria, and moderation.
- **Student-centered versus knowledge-centered:** respecting learner perspectives does not mean abandoning disciplinary standards.
- **Feedback quantity versus usability:** more comments do not help unless timely, understandable, and acted upon.
- **Technology opportunity versus substitution:** tools can expand feedback and practice, but can also remove the very cognitive work intended to develop capability.
