# Evidence Caveats and Interpretation Rules

## 1. A mean effect is not a guaranteed local effect

Average results combine settings, learners, outcomes, and implementations. Ask what produced heterogeneity and whether the local design reproduces the mechanism.

## 2. Broad labels conceal important variation

“Active learning,” “feedback,” “collaboration,” “technology,” “inquiry,” and “homework” are not single treatments. Specify:
- student task;
- instructor action;
- timing and dosage;
- guidance;
- accountability;
- feedback;
- outcome;
- learner expertise;
- context.

## 3. Statistical significance is not educational importance

Interpret magnitude, uncertainty, baseline risk, duration, transfer, cost, feasibility, and consequences. A tiny effect may matter at scale; a large short-term laboratory effect may not transfer.

## 4. Achievement is not the only outcome

Also consider persistence, belonging, self-regulation, transfer, disciplinary identity, ethical judgment, accessibility, workload, and unintended harm. Do not allow a convenient test score to redefine the course goal.

## 5. Correlation does not establish a teaching cause

Student ratings, time on task, attendance, and engagement may be useful signals but can reflect selection, prior achievement, course difficulty, identity, or grading. Use them with learning evidence and plausible causal models.

## 6. Validity belongs to interpretations and uses

A test is not simply “valid.” Evidence supports a specific interpretation for a specific use and population. New stakes, formats, assistance conditions, or populations may require new evidence.

## 7. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient

A score can be consistently wrong, narrow, or biased. Increase consistency without reducing the construct to what is easiest to score.

## 8. Short interventions are context-dependent

Mindset, belonging, and value interventions can work when they address a real local barrier and the environment affords the promised opportunity. Messaging cannot compensate for hostile structures or inadequate teaching.

## 9. Neuroscience rarely selects a classroom technique directly

Use neuroscience to understand constraints and mechanisms. Prefer educational and cognitive evidence for choosing instructional implementations.

## 10. Desirable difficulty is not arbitrary difficulty

Productive difficulty induces retrieval, discrimination, generation, or transfer at an attainable level. Confusing instructions, inaccessible media, excessive workload, and hidden prerequisites are not desirable.

## 11. Learner preference is not learning effectiveness

Students may prefer fluent explanations, rereading, or easier practice. Measure delayed performance and transfer; explain why effective practice can feel difficult.

## 12. Equity effects depend on implementation

A method that improves averages may widen, narrow, or leave gaps unchanged. Examine who can participate, what preparation is assumed, how groups are structured, and how outcomes are disaggregated.

## 13. Technology is not a causal agent by itself

Learning depends on what students do with a tool, what guidance and feedback surround it, and what work the tool replaces. “Uses AI” or “uses video” is not an instructional mechanism.

## 14. AI capability changes invalidate brittle designs

Do not base assessment on a model's current inability. Design around learning evidence, process, verification, changed conditions, and proportionate independent assurance.

## 15. Local evaluation must be proportionate and ethical

Do not run an underpowered pseudo-experiment and overclaim causality. Use feasible improvement evidence, document implementation, protect student privacy, and seek ethics review when research dissemination or risk warrants it.
