# Example — Gateway STEM Course Redesign

## Context

**Course:** Introductory Biology for majors\
**Students:** 280 first-year students; wide preparation range; 6 TAs\
**Problem:** Students can repeat terminology but struggle to explain mechanisms, choose among models, and transfer ideas to unfamiliar biological systems. DFW rates and achievement gaps are high.\
**Constraints:** Three 50-minute meetings, one lab, common final, fixed core content, limited marking capacity.

## Design diagnosis

The original course is dominated by slide-based exposition, chapter-aligned homework, two high-stakes midterms, and a final. The likely causal chain is:

1. students receive large amounts of information;
2. practice is mostly recognition and end-of-chapter blocked problems;
3. misconceptions remain hidden until exams;
4. students infer that fluent rereading equals mastery;
5. assessment requires mechanistic explanation and application that were not repeatedly practised.

The redesign therefore prioritizes **high structure, cumulative retrieval, active reasoning, worked examples, formative evidence, and transparent practice**, not simply “more activities.”

## Course capabilities

By the end, students can:

1. explain biological phenomena using interacting molecular, cellular, organismal, and evolutionary mechanisms;
2. interpret data and distinguish evidence from inference;
3. select and revise a biological model in light of anomalous evidence;
4. design a controlled investigation and justify its limits;
5. transfer core principles—energy, information, regulation, variation, and selection—to unfamiliar systems.

## Assessment architecture

| Evidence | Purpose | Frequency/stakes | Outcomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entry diagnostic | prerequisites/misconceptions; no grade | week 1 | all |
| Cumulative retrieval quiz | interrupt forgetting and calibrate study | weekly, lowest two dropped, 8% | 1–3 |
| In-class concept questions | hinge evidence and peer instruction | every meeting, participation only | 1–3 |
| Data interpretation notebooks | guided-to-independent practice | 6 short submissions, 12% | 2–3 |
| Lab investigation cycle | design, evidence, revision | staged, 20% | 2–4 |
| Two midterms | individual assurance and cumulative application | 30% | 1–4 |
| Integrative final case | transfer to a novel system | 25% | 1–5 |
| Exam wrappers/corrections | metacognition and relearning | required for quiz/midterm feedback | all |

## Weekly learning cycle

### Before class
- 20–30 minutes of focused reading/video with an advance organizer.
- Three-item retrieval task: one prior concept, one prediction, one “most confusing distinction.”
- Instructor reviews response patterns before class.

### During class
1. **Cumulative retrieval (4 minutes).** Individual response; no notes.
2. **Selective explanation/modeling (8–10 minutes).** One organizing model, not a chapter summary.
3. **Concept question (8 minutes).** Individual commitment → peer explanation → revote → instructor synthesis.
4. **Worked data example (8 minutes).** Instructor thinks aloud about representation, evidence, and uncertainty.
5. **Guided case (12 minutes).** Teams choose a model or diagnosis and submit a rationale; one student is randomly selected to report.
6. **Hinge question and exit plan (8 minutes).** The result determines the opening of the next session.

### After class
- Short problem set mixing current and earlier concepts.
- Immediate correctness feedback for selected items; explanations released after a genuine attempt.
- One error-analysis prompt: “Which cue should have led you to a different model?”

## Example session: gene regulation

**Target:** Given an unfamiliar regulatory network and experimental data, students can predict expression changes and justify the causal pathway.

**Prior retrieval:** central dogma, binding affinity, negative feedback.\
**Misconception:** a regulatory protein “turns a gene on” regardless of concentration, location, and interacting elements.

**Hinge question:** A repressor loses its ligand-binding site but still binds DNA. Which intervention most directly restores inducible expression? Each distractor maps to a different misconception about binding, transcription, or signal transduction.

**Activity:** Groups receive four mutant phenotypes and three candidate network diagrams. They must:
- eliminate two models;
- state the decisive evidence;
- predict one unobserved condition;
- identify a result that would falsify their chosen model.

The activity is constructive and interactive because students generate and revise an explanation, not merely label a diagram.

## Scaffolding and fading

- Weeks 1–3: complete worked data interpretations with annotated decision points.
- Weeks 4–6: partially completed analyses; students select missing steps.
- Weeks 7–9: full data sets with prompts only.
- Weeks 10–12: novel cases; students choose representations and justify assumptions.

High-preparation students can use challenge extensions, but everyone completes the common target evidence.

## Access and participation

- Slides and diagrams are posted in accessible format before class.
- Color is never the only code in biological figures.
- Individual think time precedes peer discussion.
- Response systems permit anonymous all-student commitment.
- Participation credit is based on preparation and reasoning attempts, not correctness or public speaking.
- Labs provide role rotation and an individual post-lab explanation.

## GenAI policy

- AI is prohibited for weekly retrieval quizzes and controlled exams.
- AI may be used after a first attempt on selected homework to request a hint or alternative explanation.
- Students must record the prompt, verify the response against course models/sources, and explain one modification.
- AI may assist coding in the lab only after students submit a pseudocode plan and must be able to explain the final code.
- The integrative final contains individual controlled evidence, so course learning is not inferred from AI-assisted products alone.

## Feasibility

Marking is controlled through:
- auto-scored retrieval and concept questions;
- TA-calibrated analytic rubrics for six short notebook samples;
- whole-class feedback on common errors;
- staged lab checkpoints that prevent long, failed final reports;
- a shared item bank and exam blueprint.

## Evaluation plan

Compare with prior offerings using:
- common concept inventory or anchored common items;
- DFW rates and distributions, disaggregated cautiously;
- performance on novel transfer items;
- weekly time-on-task samples;
- implementation fidelity (response rate, activity completion, feedback use);
- mid-course climate and access survey;
- item analysis and student error categories.

A lower DFW rate without improved transfer would not by itself justify the redesign; both learning and participation evidence matter.
