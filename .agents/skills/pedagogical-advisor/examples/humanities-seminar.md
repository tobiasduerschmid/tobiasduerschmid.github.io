# Example — Humanities Seminar: Interpretation, Argument, and Evidence

## Context

**Course:** Modern Political Thought, 18 students, second year\
**Problem:** Discussion is lively but dominated by a few students. Essays summarize readings, quote selectively, and make claims without weighing counterevidence.\
**Goal:** Preserve open inquiry while making disciplinary standards and student development more visible.

## Consequential capabilities

Students can:
1. reconstruct an author's argument charitably before criticizing it;
2. distinguish textual evidence, historical context, inference, and evaluation;
3. compare competing interpretations and identify what evidence would discriminate between them;
4. develop an arguable thesis, address a consequential objection, and revise in response to feedback;
5. participate in interpretive disagreement with precision and respect.

## Module design: liberty and authority

**Driving question:** When does political authority make freedom possible, and when does it destroy it?

### Before seminar
- Students submit a 250-word “argument map in prose”: central claim, two reasons, one assumption, one unresolved tension.
- Each student identifies a passage that most threatens their own interpretation.
- The instructor samples misconceptions and selects three passages for comparison.

### Seminar sequence (90 minutes)

1. **Retrieval and distinction (8 min).** Without notes, students define negative and positive liberty and give a counterexample to each.
2. **Silent text annotation (7 min).** All students mark claim, evidence, and inferential move in a common paragraph.
3. **Paired reconstruction (12 min).** Partners must agree on the strongest version of the argument before critique.
4. **Structured academic controversy (30 min).** Teams advocate one interpretation, reverse and represent the opposing interpretation to its proponents' satisfaction, then synthesize conditions under which each is stronger.
5. **Instructor mini-lecture (12 min).** Historical context is introduced only where it resolves or complicates interpretations students have made visible.
6. **Individual transfer (13 min).** Students apply the framework to a contemporary policy excerpt and state where the analogy breaks down.
7. **Exit claim (8 min).** “My interpretation changed/held because...” plus one piece of evidence.

## Participation design

Participation is not scored by airtime. Evidence includes:
- pre-seminar preparation;
- accurate reconstruction of another view;
- use of textual evidence;
- building on or revising a claim;
- written synthesis and exit response.

The protocol creates individual thinking time, reduces improvisational advantage, and makes listening intellectually consequential.

## Essay sequence

### Task
Write a 2,000-word comparative interpretation for an informed public audience. Argue how two thinkers would diagnose a current tension, and identify a limit of the comparison.

### Stages
1. thesis and evidence table;
2. 500-word analytical paragraph;
3. exemplar comparison and rubric calibration;
4. peer review focused on argument/evidence only;
5. full draft;
6. instructor feedback: one global priority and two local priorities;
7. revision plus a feedback-use memo;
8. five-minute oral defense using a new passage.

### Criteria
- charitable and accurate reconstruction;
- quality and sufficiency of evidence;
- inferential coherence;
- treatment of counterargument;
- comparative insight and limits;
- organization for the stated audience;
- source integrity and prose clarity.

## GenAI design

AI may be used to:
- generate possible objections after the student has submitted an initial thesis;
- compare alternate organizational structures;
- provide sentence-level feedback after substantive revision.

AI may not:
- produce the initial textual interpretation;
- supply quotations or citations without verification;
- draft the submitted argument.

Students include a short provenance note and are responsible for every claim. The oral defense and in-class passage analysis provide independent evidence of interpretive capability.

## Formative evidence and instructor adaptation

The instructor tracks:
- recurrent confusion between context and authorial intent;
- quotations used without inferential explanation;
- failure to address disconfirming passages;
- participation concentration;
- whether peer feedback results in revision.

If more than one-third of the class misidentifies an assumption in the pre-seminar map, the session begins with a worked example rather than the planned discussion.

## Why the design is evidence-informed

It combines prior retrieval, constructive explanation, structured interaction, exemplars, practice-feedback-revision, authentic audience, and independent transfer. It avoids treating unstructured discussion as inherently inclusive or deep.
