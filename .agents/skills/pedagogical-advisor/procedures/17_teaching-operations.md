# Teaching Operations and Responsive Support

Use when the professor needs teaching work done beyond planning a course or assignment: TA onboarding, marker calibration, office hours, discussion facilitation, communication drafts, accessible participation, large-class support, feedback triage, or midcourse repair. Produce the actual agenda, script, pack, message, or decision aid requested. These protocols are operational design suggestions, not universally validated interventions.

## Select the smallest useful product

| Situation | Deliverable | Evidence that it helped |
|---|---|---|
| Markers disagree | Calibration pack with anchors and decision log | Agreement on criteria and reasons; fewer systematic scoring discrepancies |
| Office hours become answer provision | Diagnostic prompts, hint ladder, queue triage | Student performs the next step and a related task independently |
| Discussion is dominated or superficial | Facilitator run sheet with preparation, turn structure, and synthesis | More students contribute disciplinary reasoning in a visible product |
| Students repeatedly misread instructions | Revised instructions and a short announcement | Fewer recurring clarification requests and fewer avoidable submission errors |
| Participation format blocks access | Equivalent route matched to the learning target | Learners can demonstrate the same capability without an irrelevant barrier |
| Feedback arrives too late | Feedback allocation and response plan | Comments are used in an actual revision or later attempt |
| Large class loses diagnostic visibility | Sampling and escalation routine with low-stakes checks | Persistent misconceptions are identified and addressed within the available time |
| Midcourse learning or workload problem | One-change improvement cycle | Learning evidence and burden improve without an unexamined tradeoff |

## A. TA onboarding and marking calibration

1. Gather the assignment, target capabilities, rubric, key, allowed alternatives, AI conditions, and relevant course policies. Read the actual materials before writing a grading guide.
2. Select or create **de-identified** strong, weak, and borderline examples. Label synthetic examples. Include an unconventional but valid solution and a polished but conceptually flawed one when useful.
3. Ask markers to score the same samples independently before seeing the lead's judgment. Record criterion scores, located evidence, feedback, confidence, and time taken.
4. Compare discrepancies by criterion and reasoning. Resolve ambiguous descriptors, valid alternatives, partial-credit rules, dependencies between errors, and opportunities for accidental double penalties. Preserve uncertainty when the sample does not provide evidence for a judgment.
5. Create an anchor pack containing annotated samples, agreed rationale, common misconception codes, a small comment bank, and an unresolved-case route. Do not normalize everyone to one marker without examining whether that marker's judgment is sound.
6. Re-score a fresh sample. Define local review triggers proportionate to the stakes; for example, disagreement about whether the central learning goal is met should be resolved even if total scores are close. Any numerical discrepancy threshold is a local operating choice, not a universal reliability standard.
7. During marking, sample across markers and score ranges. Check drift after a substantial batch. Apply clarified decisions consistently to affected work; keep a short correction record.

Use `../templates/ta-calibration-pack.md`. For consequential psychometric claims, consult `../knowledge/12_exam-design-and-psychometrics.md`; percentage agreement alone does not establish reliable or valid grading. AI may draft comments or flag inconsistencies in approved de-identified material. The accountable instructor or authorized marker makes consequential grading decisions and checks the actual work; do not infer misconduct from style or a detector.

## B. Office hours and help queues

Use a welcoming opening: **“Show me the point where your expectation and the result diverge.”** Accept a verbal explanation or partially completed work; lack of a polished attempt is not a reason to deny help.

Diagnose before selecting the help:

| Diagnosis | Next move | Check before ending |
|---|---|---|
| Missing prerequisite | Explain one necessary idea with a small worked example | Student explains a different example |
| Misread task | Ask student to identify the output and success criterion; clarify wording | Student states the task in their own words |
| Strategy gap | Compare two plausible approaches and model the decision | Student chooses an approach for the next subproblem |
| Local execution error | Ask for a prediction and inspect the first divergent step | Student repairs and explains the cause |
| AI answer accepted without understanding | Ask for a trace, source check, counterexample, or changed input | Student verifies one claim and names an unresolved limit |
| Overload or affective barrier | Acknowledge the difficulty; narrow to one achievable next step | Student leaves with a realistic action and appropriate support route |

Hint ladder: restate goal → focus attention → offer a discriminating question → show one substep → model a small analogous example → ask for a new attempt. Move up or down according to the student's response. Do not force prolonged guessing when missing knowledge is the barrier.

For queues, collect a short topic and “what I expected / what happened” note, allow nonpublic questions, batch common technical issues, and reserve individual attention for distinct needs. Publish a reasonable help-time expectation and fallback. End each interaction with an action the learner performs, not only an explanation they heard. Use recurring questions to improve materials.

## C. Discussion, lab, and group facilitation

Build a run sheet containing:

- a disciplinary question that requires a decision, evidence, interpretation, or comparison;
- quiet individual preparation with a concrete note or prediction;
- a contribution structure appropriate to the group, such as paired comparison before open discussion;
- roles only when they create useful responsibilities; rotate reasoning and reporting work so one student does not permanently operate the tool;
- prompts that press for explanation: “What evidence supports that?”, “What would change your decision?”, “Where do these accounts disagree?”;
- a facilitator synthesis distinguishing accepted conclusions, defensible alternatives, and unresolved questions;
- an individual exit response that reveals whether students can use the idea.

Prepare responses to predictable failure modes. If one voice dominates, invite written contributions or structured turns. If groups agree quickly, introduce a relevant counterexample. If talk is socially lively but conceptually thin, require a claim-evidence-decision product. If a response is incorrect, ask for the reasoning, acknowledge any valid component, and test the specific claim. Do not publicly diagnose learners or demand personal disclosure as the price of participation.

For AI-assisted group work, have students commit to a view before consulting a model when that serves the outcome; require a reasoned accept/reject decision about one model suggestion. A rotating “AI operator” should not become the only student doing substantive work.

## D. Student communication drafts

Draft the requested message with the known audience, course tone, dates, actions, and constraints. If a date or policy is unknown, use a conspicuous placeholder rather than inventing one. Use a clear subject and explain:

1. what students need to know;
2. what they should do, where, and by when;
3. why it matters for their work;
4. how to obtain help or an accessible alternative.

For feedback or difficult messages, cite the observed work or behavior, explain the relevant criterion, give a feasible next step, and avoid claims about character, motivation, diagnosis, or guilt. For assignment clarification, identify which instruction changes and whether earlier submissions are affected. For AI policy, use concrete allowed and disallowed examples consistent with the course's actual rule.

Prepare drafts freely when requested. Sending, posting, contacting students, changing official grades, or granting institutional exceptions requires authorization appropriate to that action; a request to draft is not a request to transmit. Use privacy-preserving examples and avoid copying identifiable student work into unapproved external tools.

## E. Access, accommodations, and participation

Identify the capability being taught or assessed before changing its format. Separate barriers caused by the delivery method from requirements essential to the capability. Offer usable text, alternative diagram descriptions, captions, navigable documents, explicit instructions, and flexible participation routes where appropriate to the task.

Examples: accept a written critique alongside spoken discussion when reasoning is the target; retain an appropriate spoken demonstration when oral communication itself is being assessed and use the established accommodation process. Provide a static model-output packet if live AI access is not essential to the target. Do not require a paid AI account or unnecessary personal-data disclosure to participate.

Implement confirmed accommodation requirements as supplied. Do not infer a disability, ask for diagnostic details, invent eligibility rules, or substitute a general design recommendation for an individual accommodation. When exact institutional requirements matter and are unavailable, identify the narrow missing policy information while completing useful accessible materials. Consult current authoritative policy if advising on a changing legal or institutional requirement.

## F. Large classes and feedback triage

Use `../tools/workload-model.md` to budget time. Include calibration, administration, exceptions, and the student's revision time. Replacing one feedback mode with another should preserve the intended learning signal.

1. Collect a brief common response at an instructional decision point. Use anonymous aggregation or a small purposeful sample when collecting every response is infeasible; do not generalize a convenience sample to every learner.
2. Sort needs into **whole-class pattern**, **small-group need**, and **individual concern**. Reconcile automated classifications with actual samples before acting.
3. Give a worked correction for a common error; a targeted practice set or clinic for a subgroup; and individual feedback where the issue or stakes demand it.
4. Make the next action explicit: revise one paragraph, re-run one test with an explanation, compare a solution, or answer a transfer question. Allocate time for it.
5. Recheck the target capability. Track unresolved misconceptions and who is repeatedly missing support without exposing individuals to the class.

For 240 students and a five-minute marking budget, one pass already requires 20 staff hours before calibration or administration. If the budget cannot support individual comments on every task, use fewer high-value checkpoints, criterion-based sampling, calibrated peer comparison, and whole-class feedback with individual escalation. Do not quietly assume unpaid TA overtime or treat automated scores as error-free.

## G. Midcourse improvement

Choose one consequential bottleneck. Ask students for concrete information such as “Which step takes longer than you expected?” and “Show a point where the instructions admit two interpretations.” Combine this with a short learning probe and samples of work. Satisfaction and confidence are useful signals about experience, not direct measures of learning.

Write a small change hypothesis: **“If we [specific change], students should [observable improvement], because [mechanism]. We will check [evidence] by [time].”** Choose a comparable follow-up task, record whether the change was implemented, and monitor burden and access. Explain to students what changed and the reason. If the evidence is a single cohort or before/after comparison, describe an association or local improvement signal rather than a causal effect.
