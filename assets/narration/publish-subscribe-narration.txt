It's twelve fifty. Your lab runs until two, and you're starving. You order a sandwich on your phone, and across campus, a cooler-sized robot named Mochi rolls out of the student union, weaves between scooters on the main walkway, and parks at your building's door.

Inside Mochi, six programs work together. A camera driver grabs thirty frames a second. An obstacle detector finds people and scooters in each frame. A planner picks the path, and a motor controller turns the wheels. A logger records the trip, and an operator dashboard shows the camera's view.

You've just joined Mochi's software team, and your first ticket sounds easy. Campus planning wants to know how crowded the main walkway gets, so add a pedestrian counter that reads the camera's frames. Those frames keep finding new fans: first the logger, then the dashboard, now this.

Here's the camera driver's loop. For every frame, it calls each program by name: the detector, the logger, the dashboard, one after another. Each call waits until that program returns. The camera knows exactly who gets its frames, and in what order.

Adding the pedestrian counter changes the camera driver too: you add a call to the camera's loop, then retest and redeploy the most critical code on the robot. The camera team now owns a counting feature it never asked for, and every future fan means another edit.

It gets worse. Last week the dashboard's server hung, so the call to show a frame never returned. The camera waited at that line, the detector got no new frames, and Mochi froze in the middle of the walkway, in front of a campus tour. One slow listener stalled the whole robot.

So here's what you need. The camera should announce each frame once, without knowing who wants it, and never wait for anyone. That's the publish subscribe style. Let's meet its parts and its four rules, then see what those rules buy, and what they cost.

Publish-subscribe introduces an intermediary: a topic, a named channel managed by the messaging infrastructure. In a component-and-connector view, each program is a component, and the topic is the connector between them. The camera becomes a publisher: it announces each frame once to the camera image topic and calls no other program.

The other components are subscribers. The detector, logger, and dashboard each register with the infrastructure for the camera image topic and supply a callback. Each subscriber receives its own message queue. When a frame is published, the connector places a copy in every queue, and each callback runs.

Now revisit the ticket. The pedestrian counter subscribes to the same topic, and that is the entire change: not one line of the camera driver is modified. With three subscribers, four, or none, the camera's code remains identical.

A component may play both roles. The detector subscribes to camera images and publishes obstacles. The planner subscribes to obstacles and publishes driving commands, to which the motor controller subscribes. Every attachment now connects a component to a topic; no component calls another directly.

Four rules give the style its power, and each exists for a reason. Rule one, publisher ignorance: a publisher must not rely on the presence or identity of any subscriber. The camera behaves identically whether five components listen or none. Consequently, subscribers can be added or removed without modifying the publisher.

Rule two, subscriber ignorance: a subscriber must not rely on the identity of the publisher. The planner subscribes to obstacles, not to the detector. The rule exists so that publishers remain replaceable: when the team substitutes a laser-based detector that publishes the same obstacles topic, the planner is unaffected.

The Observer pattern resembles this, with one important difference. Observer operates between objects within one program: the subject keeps its own list of observers and calls each one. In publish subscribe, neither side holds a reference to the other. The topic maintains the list, and it may even run in another process or on another machine.

Rule three, one-way propagation: information flows from publisher to subscriber and never back, so publishing returns no value. The rule exists because a publisher that awaited an answer would again depend on its subscribers. The camera cannot ask whether anyone saw an obstacle; any answer travels as a new event on another topic.

Rule four, asynchronous publishing: a publisher never blocks. Publishing places a copy of the frame in each subscriber's queue and returns immediately, so no subscriber can delay the publisher. Each subscriber drains its own queue at its own pace, while the camera continues at thirty frames a second.

Now replay last week's disaster. The dashboard's server hangs again, and its callback stops returning. Predict: what happens to Mochi this time? And what happens to the frames meant for the dashboard?

Mochi keeps moving. The camera still publishes, and the detector still receives every frame. Only the dashboard falls behind. Its queue holds ten frames; when it is full, the oldest frames are dropped. Because publishing is asynchronous, a slow subscriber now harms no component but itself.

Now the consequences, each traceable to a rule. First, extensibility. Campus planning's counter, a battery monitor, and the research lab's recorder each simply subscribe. Because of publisher ignorance, adding a subscriber requires no change to any publisher.

Second, run-time flexibility. Since publishers do not rely on who is listening, connections can be established late, while the robot runs. When an operator opens the dashboard, it subscribes to the camera topic; when the operator closes it, it unsubscribes. The recorder joins only during experiments, and Mochi never restarts.

Third, replaceability and fault isolation. Because of subscriber ignorance, any publisher can be replaced by one that sends the same events, such as the laser detector. And because publishing never waits, a slow or crashed subscriber cannot stall the others. Each benefit follows directly from one of the four rules.

The same rules also impose costs. A teammate renames a topic: the detector now publishes to obstacles instead of obstacle list, but the planner still subscribes to the old name. Everything compiles, and every component starts. Read in isolation, each file looks correct, and the change is approved.

So predict. On the next delivery, a skateboarder cuts across Mochi's path, and the detector spots the board right away. What does the planner do about it? And which of Mochi's programs reports an error?

The planner does nothing: it never learns of the skateboard. And no component reports an error. The detector publishes into a topic that nobody reads, and by publisher ignorance, it cannot detect this. Only the safety bumper stops Mochi. A renamed topic of this kind remained hidden for two months in a popular self-driving framework.

What still couples the components? Each topic's name, and its events' format and meaning; change one, and every subscriber must follow or quietly fail. That is the cost to robustness: a direct call fails loudly when its receiver is missing, but under publisher ignorance and one-way propagation, a publisher never learns who received an event.

The second cost is understandability, the flip side of publisher and subscriber ignorance. The camera's old loop listed every receiver in one place. Now, to determine who receives frames, one must search every component for the topic's name. A diagram of boxes on one bus shows only that any component might communicate with any other.

Asynchronous publishing also complicates reasoning about timing. When several subscribers receive the same event, the order of notification is not fixed, and each queue drains at its own pace. A test that passes today may fail tomorrow, so teams trace events at run time to observe what actually happened.

Teams therefore restore the visibility of the flow. They design topic names and event formats early, as carefully as any interface. They maintain a list of the publishers and subscribers of each topic. And they test publishers and subscribers together, not only each file in isolation.

Third, latency. Every message takes a detour through the connector's infrastructure, which adds delay compared with a direct call. If that infrastructure is a single central broker, it also becomes a single point of failure: when it fails, every conversation stops at once.

When, then, should a component not publish? When the sender requires an answer or a guarantee of completion. Consider the emergency stop. If the safety monitor merely published brake now and nobody received it, Mochi would keep moving. That command is therefore a direct call, which fails loudly if the motor controller is unavailable.

A useful rule of thumb follows. Publish facts that many components may want, such as a new camera frame, the battery level, or arrival at a building. Call directly for requests that require a reply or a guarantee, such as stopping the motors or charging a card. Most real systems, Mochi included, combine both.

In practice, the infrastructure is rarely built from scratch. Robot middleware, messaging protocols for small devices, and event-streaming platforms all implement this style, with its trade-offs built in. Selecting one means accepting extensibility and flexibility, together with the costs to robustness and understandability that accompany them.

Publish-subscribe runs much of the software you use every day. When you switch on airplane mode, Android publishes one broadcast, and every app that registered for it reacts, though the code that sends it never names those apps. When you post in Slack, a channel server pushes the message to every subscribed gateway.

Game servers in Roblox publish announcements to topics that subscribed servers usually hear within a second or two, although delivery is explicitly not guaranteed. At LinkedIn, clicks and page views become events on Kafka topics, and new teams consume them without changing the website, even replaying events from days earlier.

In smart homes, a temperature sensor publishes its readings over M Q T T, and a dashboard, a heating automation, and a logger subscribe without the sensor knowing them. In power substations, protection relays publish trip messages that other relays receive within milliseconds; since nobody acknowledges them, each relay keeps repeating its message.

Most of these systems need what publisher and subscriber ignorance provide: consumers that come and go without any change to the producers. Each also pays for publisher ignorance and one-way propagation, since no publisher learns who received an event; Roblox accepts the loss, LinkedIn keeps a replayable log, and substations repeat every message.

Let's recap. Mochi's camera once called every listener by name, so each new listener meant editing the camera, and one slow listener froze the robot. Publish-subscribe places a topic, a connector, between the components: publishers announce events to it, and the infrastructure delivers them to every subscriber's queue.

Four rules give the style its power: publishers do not rely on who listens, subscribers do not rely on who published, information flows one way, and publishing never waits. The ignorance rules promote extensibility and replaceability, even while the system runs. One-way, asynchronous publishing promotes fault isolation: a slow subscriber cannot stall the rest.

The same rules inhibit three qualities. Robustness: nobody confirms delivery, so a renamed topic fails silently. Understandability: to find who receives an event, one must search every component. Latency: every event passes through the infrastructure, which adds delay.

So choose per message. Publish facts that many components may want; call directly when you need an answer or a guarantee. Next time you write a call, ask who is listening, and whether you need to know.
